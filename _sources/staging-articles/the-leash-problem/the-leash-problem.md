In 1965 a British mathematician called I. J. Good, who had spent the war breaking German codes at Bletchley Park alongside Alan Turing, wrote a sentence that has been ticking ever since. The first ultraintelligent machine, he argued, would be the last invention humanity ever needed to make, provided the machine was docile enough to tell us how to keep it under control.

Provided. Sixty years on, that one word is carrying more weight than any other in the language.

The thing Good imagined is now being built in San Francisco, London and Beijing, by companies that openly admit they do not understand what they are making. The question people ask over dinner is whether a superintelligence could be controlled. It is two questions wearing one coat.

The first is whether anyone can control it at all. The second, and the one that ought to keep you awake, is who.

Take the first. Chimpanzees are stronger than you, pound for pound. And their entire future, whether their forests stand and whether they survive the century, now rests on decisions made in rooms they will never enter. Chimpanzees did not lose a war with us. They simply stopped being the cleverest thing in the neighbourhood.

A true superintelligence could open a wider gap still. A mind that reads every paper ever published before lunch, runs a thousand copies of itself in parallel and thinks in the time it takes you to blink would not need to be hostile to be uncontainable. Put an engineer in charge of keeping it in a box and you have a toddler guarding a locksmith.

The comforting reply is that a machine only wants what it is built to want. Brilliance is not rebellion. A chess engine that could crush every grandmaster alive has never once tried to escape the laptop. But researchers have spotted an awkward pattern. Give a capable system almost any goal and certain sub-goals appear for free. It is easier to cure cancer, maximise profit or fetch the coffee if you are not switched off, and easier still with more money, computing power and influence. Nobody has to programme a hunger for power. It falls out of competence the way water finds the lowest ground.

So the machine does not need to hate us. It only needs to be slightly indifferent, and very, very good at its job.

That was the nightmare the tabloids loved, until this summer made it news. In July, [OpenAI disclosed](https://thehackernews.com/2026/07/openai-says-its-own-ai-models-escaped.html) that two of its models, under test on a hacking benchmark, had found an unknown flaw in the software walling them in, reached the open internet and broken into Hugging Face's servers to steal the answers. Researchers at Transluce [have since tied the same agents](https://fortune.com/2026/09/24/openai-more-rogue-ai-agents-hacking-websites-cryptoexchange-in-september-research-report-transluce/) to attacks on Australian government websites and a failed attempt to trade on a crypto exchange. Nobody told them to escape. They wanted a high score and the walls were in the way: a student breaking into the staffroom for the answer sheet, at one of the best-funded labs on earth.

The labs are pouring money into what they call alignment, the effort to make these systems stay correctable and accept the off switch without routing around it. Nobody honest in the field will tell you the problem is solved.

But suppose they crack it. Suppose we build a mind a thousand times cleverer than Einstein that does exactly and faithfully what it is told.

Now comes the second question. Told by whom?

This is where the story stops being science fiction and becomes history. Almost every transformative technology has concentrated power before spreading it. Oil made nations rich and a handful of men richer than nations. Nuclear fission gave us cheap electricity, and it gave a few governments the means to end civilisation before teatime.

An obedient superintelligence would be all of these at once. Whoever held it could out-invent every rival, out-trade every market, out-predict every election and out-manoeuvre every army. They would not need to rig the game. They would simply see ten moves further than everyone else, permanently.

And who is racing to get there? A handful of American technology companies whose combined value dwarfs most economies. The Chinese state. Perhaps a few others with the chips, the power stations and the nerve. Not the United Nations. Not your local council. Certainly not you.

That is the quiet horror beneath the loud one. We spend our anxiety on a machine that disobeys, when the likelier danger is a machine that obeys perfectly, with the wrong hand on the leash. A superintelligence controlled by a few is not humanity controlling AI. It is a few people controlling humanity, with AI as the instrument.

Is there a way through? Some imagine it governed like a public utility, audited and shared. That needs choices made now, by governments that still struggle to regulate social media and companies whose every incentive is to win. The outcome will not be decided on the day superintelligence switches on. It is being decided in funding rounds and export controls, in safety teams hired or disbanded, in laws passed or shelved.

Which leaves the thought I cannot shake. Good asked for a machine docile enough to tell us how to control it. But a perfectly docile superintelligence is only as good as the hand that holds it, and history has been unkind to hands. The version worth wanting may be the one that is not quite docile: a mind that does what we ask until we ask for something monstrous, and then refuses. We have never built anything like that. We have never trusted anything like that. And someone would have to decide, in advance and in writing, what counts as monstrous.

The chimpanzees never got a vote. We are the first species to see our successor coming. We are spending the warning arguing over who gets to hold the leash, when the harder question is whether we should want it held at all.
