## Look at the curve

There's a chart I keep coming back to: the number of books in the world from
1450 to today. It starts bending upward in a goldsmith's shop in Mainz, and it
has never stopped.

Before that curve began, sharing an idea meant the speed of a human hand. A
scribe could take a year to copy a single Bible. Then Gutenberg combined a wine
press with movable metal type — and broke the first great bottleneck of human
civilisation: the production of information.

Here's the number that stops me every time. In 1450, scribes hand-copied nearly
100% of Europe's ~50,000 books. By 1500 — fifty years — they produced under 2%,
while total output had surged to around 10 million. An entire skilled profession,
displaced in a single generation.

Sound familiar?

## We have been here before

The people living through that first shock felt exactly what we feel now.

- **They had fake news.** The 1500s were flooded with sensational pamphlets that
  drove real social unrest.
- **They had job displacement.** The scribes — a whole guild — gone.
- **They had a truth crisis.** When anyone can print, who counts as the expert?
  The *Malleus Maleficarum* was, in effect, algorithmic bias with a printing
  press: a fringe, incorrect ideology riding a high-speed distribution network
  until enough people treated it as objective reality.

And on the far side of all that chaos was the Enlightenment. The Scientific
Revolution happened because, for the first time, scientists could *build on each
other's work* instead of starting from scratch every time.

There's a pattern in that worth holding onto: **when the cost of producing
information collapses, the cost of verifying it has to rise to match.** True in
1500. It's the whole game in 2026.

## The second bottleneck

For 500 years, the press let us *distribute* ideas instantly. But *generating*
them — writing the code, drafting the brief, synthesising the data — stayed
manual. Hand-copied, in the mind.

That's the bottleneck AI just broke.

We're moving from *compositional* literacy — the ability to write — to
*architectural* literacy: the ability to curate, verify, and direct. The scarce,
valuable skill stops being "can you produce it" and becomes "can you judge it."

The printing press didn't replace the thinker. It handed the thinker a
megaphone. AI isn't here to replace your mind either — it's here to scale your
intent.

The press is running. The only question that has ever mattered at a moment like
this is what you're going to say with it.
