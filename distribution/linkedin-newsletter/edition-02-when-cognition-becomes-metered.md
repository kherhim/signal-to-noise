**Title:** When cognition becomes metered

A frontier model reviews a standard NDA for about 40 pence.

A paralegal on £45k does the same review for roughly £24 of loaded time — plus
the two-day queue in legal's inbox that's quietly holding up the deal. Sixty-fold
cheaper.

And almost entirely beside the point.

Because 40p is the cost of the *tokens*, not the cost of the *outcome*. The
model's output is probabilistic, so someone senior still spot-checks it — ten
minutes of a solicitor's time, £15 or more. Add the escalation path for the
reviews it gets wrong, and the one-off cost of designing the checking process at
all, and a genuinely trustworthy review lands somewhere around £8–15. Still far
cheaper. Still dramatically faster. But the number that mattered was never on the
token invoice.

That gap — between what a thing costs and what a trustworthy outcome costs — is
the whole subject of this piece.

And it just got the cleanest demonstration imaginable. Earlier this month,
OpenAI's CFO Sarah Friar published five lessons from building an AI-native
finance function — a team with the closest thing on earth to an unmetered
supply of cognition. Her final lesson? Measure "value per unit of
intelligence": count what the work really cost, "including employee time,
review, and rework", because "buying more seats or using more tokens doesn't
tell you much". When the meter reads nearly nothing and the costs that remain
are review and rework, you've learned where the cost was hiding all along.

## A third pool of capacity

For most of the modern corporate era, a firm's productive capacity came in two
forms, and finance built its vocabulary around them. People: headcount,
utilisation, attrition. Software: licences, assets, depreciation. Hire, buy,
amortise.

There's now a third pool. If people are embodied judgement and software is frozen
logic, metered cognition sits between them — configurable, rented per unit,
endlessly elastic, and (in ways I come back to) not actually yours. Pure variable
opex, expensed as consumed.

Notice what that does to the accounts. A firm can materially expand its thinking
capacity and leave *no trace* on the balance sheet. No asset, no depreciation
schedule, no headcount line — just a subscription and a usage charge dissolving
into operating costs. The capacity is real; the accounting is nearly silent.

## The brake is gone

Under the old model, more thinking meant more people, and people came with
friction — hiring, training, management span. That friction was annoying, but it
was also a brake. Nobody accidentally hired forty analysts.

Metered cognition has almost no friction, so the brake is gone. Cheap inputs obey
Jevons' logic: when something gets dramatically cheaper, consumption expands to
swallow the saving. Suddenly there's a machine-written post-mortem on every
ticket, a summary of every meeting, a report nobody reads. The token bill grows;
the stock of decisions actually improved by it doesn't.

The binding constraint shifts from *availability* to *design*. Not "can we afford
this capacity?" but "where in the system does it belong, and which outcomes
justify any spend at all?" That's a harder question, and a rate card can't answer
it.

## Capacity you don't control

Here's the part that gets the least airtime and deserves the most.

When capacity is people, you have levers — retention, contracts, culture. When
it's a perpetual software licence, version 11 keeps behaving like version 11.
Metered cognition offers neither. The vendor can reprice it. They can deprecate
the model your workflow was tuned to — on cycles of months, not years. They can
change its behaviour with a mid-quarter update, without asking. The
characteristics of your capacity can shift without your consent, and there's no
version you can pin and own.

Layer the probabilistic output on top and the right mental model is obvious: this
is a sole-supplier dependency for a critical input, and it should be governed like
one. Second-sourcing. An abstraction layer so a model swap is a config change, not
a rebuild. Fallback paths for the processes that can't tolerate an outage.
Supply-chain discipline, applied to thinking itself. Very few firms have made that
connection yet.

It's also the one part of this argument you will never read on a vendor's blog.
No supplier's CFO will advise you to second-source their product — and OpenAI's
finance team is the only one on earth with no AI vendor risk, because they *are*
the vendor. The silence on this section is worth noticing. It's where I'm going
next.

Once cognition can be bought in units, the cost structure of the firm becomes,
partly, a philosophical choice. You're deciding what you believe human minds are
*for*.
