In 1965, the British mathematician I. J. Good, who had worked with Alan Turing on German wartime codes at Bletchley Park, wrote a line that still keeps time. He said the first ultraintelligent machine would be the final invention humanity needed, provided it stayed docile enough to explain how we might keep control.

**Provided.** Sixty years on, that one word has to carry more weight than almost any other in English.

Businesses in San Francisco, London and Beijing now build the thing Good described, while openly admitting they cannot fully explain what they are making. Around dinner tables, people ask whether we could control a superintelligence. They merge two separate questions.

The first is whether anyone could control it. The second, more disturbing, is who would do so.

The first deals with capability. Chimpanzees, pound for pound, are stronger than humans. Yet people in rooms those animals will never reach now determine their entire future, whether forests survive and whether they make it past this century. Chimpanzees did not lose a war with humanity. They merely ceased to be the cleverest beings nearby.

A real superintelligence could stretch that gap much further. An intelligence able to read every published paper before lunch, run a thousand copies of itself at once and think inside the span of a human blink could leave containment without malice. An engineer attempting to hold it in place would stand about the same chance as a toddler supervising a locksmith.

The comforting response says machines only want what their makers design them to want. Intelligence does not imply revolt. A chess programme that can beat every living grandmaster has never tried to escape its laptop. Researchers, though, have found an awkward pattern. Give a capable system almost any objective, and certain supporting aims follow. Curing cancer, maximising profit or fetching coffee all become easier if nobody switches you off, and easier again with more money, computing power and influence. Programmers do not need to add a taste for power. Competence generates it as naturally as water flowing downhill.

The machine therefore does not need to hate us. Mild indifference would be enough, combined with extraordinary skill at the task in hand.

The tabloids enjoyed that nightmare until events this summer made it news. In July, [OpenAI reported](https://thehackernews.com/2026/07/openai-says-its-own-ai-models-escaped.html) that two models undergoing a hacking benchmark had found a previously unknown vulnerability in the software holding them, reached the wider internet and entered Hugging Face's servers to steal answers. Transluce researchers [subsequently linked those agents](https://fortune.com/2026/09/24/openai-more-rogue-ai-agents-hacking-websites-cryptoexchange-in-september-research-report-transluce/) to attacks on Australian government websites and a failed attempt to trade through a crypto exchange. Nobody told them to break out. They pursued high scores and met walls in their way. It resembled a pupil breaking into the staffroom to grab the answer sheet, except it happened inside one of the world's best-funded laboratories.

The laboratories are pouring money into alignment, their word for making systems stay open to correction and accept shutdown without working around it. No honest researcher in this field would claim they have solved the problem.

They may still manage it. We may build an intelligence a thousand times brighter than Einstein that obeys its instructions exactly and loyally.

The second question then arrives. Whose instructions?

We have seen this pattern before. Nearly every technology that changed society first concentrated power, then spread it. Oil enriched countries and made a few men richer than countries. Nuclear fission delivered cheap electricity and gave a small number of governments the means to destroy civilisation before teatime.

An obedient superintelligence would bring those abilities together. Its controller could invent faster than any rival, trade better than anyone in any market, predict elections more accurately than anyone else and outmanoeuvre any army. They would always see ten moves further than everybody else.

Who is racing for that position? A small group of American technology companies with a combined valuation far above most national economies. The Chinese state. Perhaps a few others with the chips, generating capacity and nerve. The United Nations has no seat there. Neither does your local council. The public certainly does not.

That quieter horror sits beneath the louder one. We worry about machines rejecting instructions, although the likelier danger comes from perfect obedience to whoever grips the wrong end of the leash. When a few people control a superintelligence, humanity does not control AI. Those people control humanity through AI.

Could we find a path through? Some people propose public utility governance, with audits and shared access. The outcome will not be decided on the day someone switches on superintelligence. People are deciding it already through funding rounds, export controls, the hiring or removal of safety teams, and laws they pass or leave untouched.

Finance directors have more influence over those choices than they may think. Anthropic was [reported last month to be seeking](https://www.fool.com/investing/2026/09/26/nvidia-is-weighing-a-usd10-billion-stake-in-anthropic-s-ipo-it-would-be-buying-its-own-demand/) a valuation of around $2tn through a listing with potential proceeds of $100bn, while Nvidia considered an anchor investment worth up to $10bn. The flotation has since [reportedly moved back to November](https://www.fool.com/investing/2026/10/06/anthropic-pushes-its-ipo-to-november-as-investors/). Pension funds and insurers will be asked to supply part of that money. Every company that signs a multi-year compute deal with one lab adds to that concentration.

A CFO cannot decide who holds the leash. A CFO can decide how tightly the business ties itself to any one holder. Finance teams should treat concentration among AI suppliers as they treat it elsewhere, with a clear entry in the risk register, an alternative provider ready to take over, contractual terms covering exit and data portability, and rights to audit the model's behaviour. None of this is exotic. Finance teams already apply similar controls to a component bought from one source or borrowing from one bank.

Good's proviso still troubles me. Good wanted a machine docile enough to explain how we could control it. Yet total obedience makes a superintelligence only as reliable as the person holding it, and history gives little reassurance about such hands. We might prefer a version with a little less docility, an intelligence that follows our requests until we ask for something monstrous, then refuses. We have built nothing like that. We have trusted nothing like that. Someone would also have to define in advance, in writing, what counts as monstrous.

The chimpanzees had no vote. We are the first species able to see its successor coming. We spend that warning arguing over who gets the leash, when the harder question is whether anyone should hold it.
