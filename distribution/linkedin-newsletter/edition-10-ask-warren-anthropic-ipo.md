Anthropic submitted its second amended prospectus to the SEC on 10 November and started its roadshow that day. The cover gives a range of $70 to $78 a share for 825 million Class A shares ([Anthropic S-1/A](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/2130357/000162828026072484/anthropicpbc-sx1ax2.htm)). It leaves two lines empty: the final price and the underwriting discount. The document includes nine months of revenue, five share classes, the lock-ups and a long list of banks. The company still has not stated what the banks will receive.

The market produced that range. The S&P 500 dropped 4.4% on Thursday 5 November, its steepest fall since April 2025. The midterms had given Democrats both houses of Congress, while several incoming governors said they would pause data-centre building ([CNN](https://cnn.com/2026/11/05/markets/asian-markets-us-rout-ai-investment-intl-hnk)). By Friday, investors cited by Bloomberg and the Wall Street Journal put the valuation between $1 trillion and $1.3 trillion, down from the $2 trillion figure discussed in September ([Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/technology/article/anthropics-ipo-valuation-could-plunge-to-near-1-trillion-amid-market-turmoil-153332732.html)). Kalshi put the chance of an announcement before December at 57% ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/06/anthropic-ipo-kalshi-post-midterms-ai-sell-off.html)). Four days afterwards, the top of the range put Anthropic's fully diluted value at $1.405 trillion. People familiar with the transaction told CNBC that it will price on 18 November and begin trading the following day ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/10/anthropic-sets-price-range-of-70-to-78-in-upcoming-ipo.html)).

Warren Buffett has described this moment more clearly than any banker. He once ran a public offering himself. His letters offer three tests for any capital raise a CFO approves, including this one.

## Who picks the time

Buffett used his [1992 letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/1992.html) to explain why Berkshire did better buying in the open market than buying new issues. The open market, "periodically ruled by mass folly", can sell a business worth x for half of x. New issues run on another basis:

> "The new-issue market, on the other hand, is ruled by controlling stockholders and corporations, who can usually select the timing of offerings or, if the market looks unfavorable, can avoid an offering altogether."

Sellers who choose the date do not put out bargains. "It's rare you'll find x for 1/2x here."

Anthropic holds that choice, and last week showed its value. The company proceeded at a lower valuation, with the founders choosing up to $1.4 trillion this month instead of an unknown number in the spring. Buyers will pay a price the seller accepted.

No one compels the seller to act. Anthropic held $95.6 billion in cash and another $36.3 billion in short-term investments at the end of September. Its May private round gave it a $965 billion valuation ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/26/anthropic-ipo-live-updates-ai-s-1-filing.html)). Companies with time can sell at a price that suits them.

## Who the deal is built for

Buffett issued Berkshire's B shares in 1996 partly to prevent promoters from selling high-fee trusts that copied Berkshire for small investors. He wanted owners instead of traders, and he structured the offer to deter traders ([1996 letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/1996.html)). "To blunt the enthusiasm that brokers normally have for pushing new issues - because that's where the money is - we arranged for our offering to carry a commission of only 1.5%." Buffett left the offer size open-ended, "thereby repelling the typical IPO buyer who looks for a short-term price spurt arising from a combination of hype and scarcity." Trading after the issue, his rough measure of flipping, sat well below the usual level for a new issue.

Anthropic has set most of its terms in the opposite direction. The underwriting fee remains blank. Schwab, Fidelity, Robinhood, SoFi and E*TRADE, including their apps, will place part of the offer with retail investors. People familiar with the deal told CNBC that the target sits near 20%, compared with the usual 5% to 10%. Existing holders will sell 23.6 million shares alongside the company's 801.4 million. Anthropic receives no proceeds from those shares, while the prospectus includes an "orderly distribution" for those sellers among the deal's purposes. Directors, officers and staff may sell up to 316 million shares from the first day of trading, with later tranches opening from the 27th day. The 825 million offered shares represent about 6% of the roughly 13.1 billion Class A and Class N shares outstanding after the transaction. A float this small at a valuation above $1 trillion would produce the scarcity Buffett tried to avoid.

Buffett is also a fair comparison on control, though he was no purist about it. He ran Berkshire with his own controlling stake. Anthropic takes control further. A company owned by the seven co-founders holds one Class F share, which carries 50.1% of votes on general matters. A Long-Term Benefit Trust owns Class T shares and chooses the Class T directors. Those directors will make up a board majority. Class A buyers receive one vote each and little influence. Berkshire's public holders also left control with Buffett, though they placed their trust in decades of results. Anthropic's buyers place their trust in a record counted in quarters.

## Is the listing the goal

Buffett wrote the [2000 letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/2000ar/2000letter.html) as the dot-com bubble burst, and it sets the toughest of the three tests. "Too often, an IPO, not profits, was the primary goal of a company's promoters."

Anthropic clears this test more easily than many companies. Revenue for the nine months to September reached $33.2 billion, compared with $2.5 billion a year earlier. About 85 cents from every dollar remained after the cost of revenue. Operating cash flow came in positive at $6.6 billion. The revenue and cash exist.

The test reaches lower in the income statement. The 85 cents covers only compute used to serve customers. Research and development covers work on the next model, costing $19.2 billion across the same nine months, and the company still posted an operating loss of $1.3 billion. Anthropic has committed about $900.9 billion of spending, nearly all for compute and infrastructure. Payments of $451.6 billion fall due before the end of 2030. Many contracts require take-or-pay terms, so Anthropic pays for capacity whether it uses the capacity or not. Those commitments equal roughly 25 years of sales against trailing revenue of about $35 billion.

The goal is the data centres, and the listing helps pay for them. Two weeks ago I wrote that the biggest [cash machines are borrowing](https://signal-to-noise.co/insights/the-cash-machines-are-borrowing/) to pay for the build-out. Anthropic sits at the end of that chain. Its prospectus treats public equity as another source of funding for capacity it has already agreed to buy. The filing also says that opposition to data centres has become better organised across jurisdictions, and that Anthropic's public profile may attract attention to linked projects. That risk factor has read like current news since the midterms.

## What Buffett would do

Buffett would not buy at the offer. He wrote in 1992 that "an intelligent investor in common stocks will do better in the secondary market than he will do buying new issues", and this deal offers no exception. He would study the prospectus, flag the take-or-pay commitments and wait for folly in the open market to put the business on sale at a price the sellers had not selected. If nine months of figures reflect a business this good, the business will remain good after the lock-ups expire. I made a related point about the [SpaceX, Anthropic and OpenAI listings](https://signal-to-noise.co/insights/pricing-the-future-spacex-anthropic-openai-ipos/) in May, and the past week has made the case clearer.

These three tests apply beyond this deal. Before any capital raise, a CFO acting as issuer or buyer can ask who picked the date, who the terms are meant to attract, and what the money pays for.

A CFO buying Anthropic's services can also use the third test. Suppliers carrying $900 billion of fixed commitments must keep capacity busy, and their contracts will show it. Minimum spend, multi-year terms and volume tiers shift risk to customers, as I argued in [budgeting for deflation](https://signal-to-noise.co/insights/budgeting-for-deflation/). Token prices may continue to fall. The terms around them may not.
