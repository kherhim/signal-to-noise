On Thursday 12 November, I opened a summit of finance chiefs with the keynote. I spoke about the CFO's new mandate, and about finance shifting from reporting outcomes to steering choices. I covered risk in one part of the speech, including the checks I believe every plan must clear before the board sees it.

Nvidia announced later that day that Jensen Huang had stepped down as president, chief executive and director, effective immediately ([Nvidia](https://investor.nvidia.com/news/press-release-details/2026/NVIDIA-Announces-CEO-Transition/)). He had led the company for 33 years. His departure followed a week of allegations about his private life that drew in US intelligence agencies, and I will leave those to others. The board appointed Colette Kress, CFO since 2013, as interim chief executive. She will keep the CFO role too. It also appointed Harvey Jones, a director since 1993, as the company's first-ever chair. Nvidia had become a $4 trillion company without one ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/12/nvidia-jensen-huang-ceo-live-updates.html)).

The stock began trading more than 13% down. Five days on, Kress presented third-quarter results while signing the accounts and answering for the strategy.

## What the filings said

Nvidia once identified the risk in its annual reports. Its fiscal 2002 to 2007 10-Ks said losing key staff, "particularly Jen-Hsun Huang, our President and Chief Executive Officer, would harm our business." The filings also said the company carried no key-person life insurance for anyone ([FY2006 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001045810/000104581006000014/fy2006annualreportonform10-k.htm)).

The current filing names nobody. Nvidia says it is "highly dependent on the services of our longstanding executive team" and says poor succession planning "could hinder our strategic planning, execution, and long-term success" ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/09/jensen-huang-affair-nvidia-succession.html)). The more Nvidia relied on him, the broader the wording became. The third-quarter release sets out a new risk: the company's "ability to successfully transition NVIDIA's leadership, identify and integrate a new chief executive officer" ([Nvidia](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001045810/000104581026000088/q3fy27pr.htm)). CNBC said Huang had 33 direct reports, with no obvious deputy. The other ten directors had averaged 15 years of service, compared with 7.8 for independent directors across the S&P 500. Bernstein's Stacy Rasgon summed it up in six words on air: "Jensen is Nvidia. Nvidia is Jensen."

Most companies allow risk factors to move in this direction. Lawyers widen the wording, and each reviewer approves it, since nobody can easily challenge a general statement. Naming someone would mean discussing that person. I have seen capable boards avoid such discussions for years, and I see the reason. Successful founders rarely invite questions about who follows them.

## The test I left out

The keynote set out three checks for every plan. The concentration test was one of them. Say our biggest customer, supplier, funder or system disappears tomorrow. What remains of the plan? The board can use the answer to set an approved exposure cap.

My list contained four nouns. Nvidia's week suggests a fifth belonged there. One person can create the biggest concentration in a business, yet standard risk registers seldom record it in those terms. Revenue notes identify customer concentration. Procurement reviews identify supplier concentration. Nobody records the person with 33 reporting lines and the product roadmap, until shares open 13% lower.

Markets measured the cost within hours. Nvidia's value dropped below $4 trillion at the open. At least ten analysts lowered their targets that week, while Baird downgraded the shares. In his downgrade note, John Vinh of KeyBanc warned of "a material increase in strategic, organizational, and execution risk" ([Investing.com](https://www.investing.com/news/analyst-ratings/nvidia-stock-downgraded-by-keybanc-on-ceo-succession-concerns-93CH-5021420)). Investors put a price on the missing succession plan, and it was large.

## Why the board reached for the CFO

In a crisis, boards want continuity they can describe in one sentence. A CFO with 13 years in post knows the numbers, the banks, the lenders and the investors who will call on Monday. Investors have listened to her start Nvidia's earnings calls for years. Results were five days away, so the decision made sense. She reported revenue of $115.8 billion, more than twice the level a year earlier, and guidance of $130 billion for the next quarter. The shares rose about 4% after hours.

That is a great deal for one person to carry. As interim CEO, she signs the quarter as CFO, explains it as chief executive, and runs the company while the board decides whether to make the role permanent. The chair brought in Heidrick & Struggles for the search. In a written statement, Huang said he would "be honored to answer these calls and return as CEO". The board said it does not plan to reinstate him ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/17/nvidia-nvda-earnings-report-q3-2027.html)). Dan Ives expects her to remain for about a year before an outsider takes over. Others back Ian Buck, who runs the hyperscale and high-performance computing business.

Finance chiefs can put the same question to their companies. If my CEO left on Thursday and the board asked me to step in, could finance operate without me on Friday? Most finance teams centre on the CFO's judgement. The close continues and the controls hold, yet decisions on capital, bank relationships and the board pack all pass through one desk. I said in the keynote that finance should control capital allocation, since most CEOs have never practised it. That argument comes with a duty. If finance wants the major decisions, it has to survive losing the person who makes them.

## Three things for Monday

I ended the keynote with three Monday actions. Nvidia's week points to three more.

Name the people in your concentration test. Put the three or four people whose sudden exit would change the plan alongside the biggest customer and biggest supplier, then bring that page to the board.

Write down who acts, and on what authority. When you agree decision rights with your CEO in writing, the big calls cross your desk before they are made. That document should also say who makes those calls if either of you is gone tomorrow.

Review your bench. Choose the person who would sign the next quarter if you could not, then hand them a real part of the work now, while nothing is on fire.

Nvidia's board needed a chair, a chief executive and a plan in one night. It found the first in a director of 33 years and the second in its CFO. The third remains unwritten.
