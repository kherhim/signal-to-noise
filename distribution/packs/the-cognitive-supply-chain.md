## P1 — "The Tuesday email" — ✅ FIRED Tue 1 Sep ~9:45am UK

The message lands on a Tuesday. The model underpinning your contract-review workflow is due for retirement; you have ninety days.

The vendor says its successor outperforms the old model across most benchmarks. That may well be right. But the note does not address the four particular tasks on which your workflow relies. Your organisation neither selected this change nor had a say in it.

Imagine a supplier of physical components taking that approach. During a contract, it replaces the part around which your production line was built, sends an email, and sets the change for ninety days away. By Thursday, procurement has mobilised: contractual provisions are triggered, alternatives enter qualification, and an executive is boarding a plane.

Sell software instead, and that identical notice reaches IT, then disappears into a renewal queue.

That is the asymmetry that matters. In the last two years, companies have largely taken on a critical supplier without managing it — for their most important purchased input: thinking. A vendor can alter its behaviour, withdraw the product or reprice it partway through a quarter, with no need to consult you.

Procurement learned to handle this problem decades ago, at the price of factories brought to a halt: specifications, dual sources, incoming inspection and change-notice clauses. Cognition has received almost none of that treatment. Model output is recorded as a tool, although its behaviour is that of a supplied component. A component earns a specification and second sources. A tool gets an invoice.

## P2 — "Four disciplines" — slot: Mon 8 Sep, 8am UK (user now on BST)

**Hook:** Your AI vendor cannot be prevented from changing its model. What you can do is know what has changed before your customers notice.

Resilience in physical supply chains came from costly experience: plants halted and quarters lost. Four disciplines from that world map almost directly onto AI dependency:

1. Second-sourcing. For every critical workflow, qualify another model — from another vendor, or at minimum another model family — then keep it live by sending a modest portion of real traffic to it. A fallback left untested will fail on the precise day you need it.

2. The abstraction layer. A model swap ought to be a configuration update, rather than a rebuild. A single internal interface determines whether a retirement notice creates an operational task or a six-month programme.

3. Incoming inspection. Run every model update through an evaluation suite drawn from your own live cases: contracts, forecasts and the edge cases that burned you previously. If you lack this, your quality control is "wait for complaints".

4. Fallback paths. Define, for each critical workflow, the degraded service, its capacity and the people required. The answer may be a second model. It may be people operating at one-tenth throughput. Work may sometimes stop — provided that choice was made beforehand.

Resilience does consume margin; the cost concern is legitimate. Procurement handles it through tiering. At most firms, perhaps five processes merit the complete discipline. The essential task is identifying those five.

## P3 — "The cognitive bill of materials" — RESERVE (hold for next model-retirement/outage news peg)

**Hook:** A plant manager can name every single-sourced component on a line from memory. Ask a COO which company processes include a model call, and a shrug is the candid response.

There is a name for that missing view: the cognitive bill of materials. It lists every process using model output; the supplier and model serving it; the failure blast radius; and the plan for a change.

This is not a complicated document. Yet most firms lack one, and concentration that remains invisible cannot be governed. No one decision introduced this dependency. It built up integration by integration and team by team, beneath every threshold subject to formal tracking.

At the same time, the input it captures is quietly turning into a top-ten supplier expense. That makes it a CFO and procurement concern, not an IT-only one. Three questions cover most board-level work: Do we have the cognitive bill of materials? Which tier-one processes rely on one supplier? When did we last test a fallback in reality, not simply review it?

An outage makes itself known. A behaviour change does not. On a monthly cycle, someone unfamiliar with your process is redesigning the component on which that process relies.

Can your firm produce its cognitive bill of materials? Most cannot.
