# Ready to post — 10 commandments for the experienced CFO

*Canonical: https://signal-to-noise.co/insights/10-commandments-experienced-cfo/*

A numbered "commandments" piece is one of the highest save-and-share formats on
LinkedIn — evergreen, scannable, and endlessly resurfaceable. The list structure
does half the work for you: it invites people to argue which one they'd cut, and
to tag the CFO they wish had read it. Finished posts below, not scaffolds. Nothing

## LinkedIn — Post 1 (the flagship — the full list)

> After a year as a CFO, someone asked me a fair question: now that you're experienced, what are your commandments?
>
> Here's my honest answer. Ten of them.
>
> 1. Always have integrity. There will be moments when a gap opens between your words and your actions. Resist it.
> 2. You may look backward, but you must think forward. Finance is not a rear-view mirror. Forecast, plan scenarios, assess risk.
> 3. Champion cross-functional collaboration. Without it, a firm optimises for local maximas, never the global one.
> 4. Embrace technology. More transactions than ever means cloud, automation and data aren't optional.
> 5. Champion financial literacy. Accounting is the language of business, yet most of the business can't speak it.
> 6. Balance short-term and long-term. Sometimes you compromise this quarter for a health you can't yet prove.
> 7. Protect and distribute resources wisely. Be the brakes that steer the F1 car through the bend, not the brakes that slow it down.
> 8. Develop and mentor. Hire leaders who hire leaders. Build a room where people can challenge you.
> 9. Listen actively. Stop listening, and you'll only hear what you already believe.
> 10. Be resilient and adaptable. Hold a crystal-clear conviction of the long term — and stay willing to change the crystal.
>
> If I had to defend just one under pressure, it's the first. Everything else assumes it.
>

**Why this works:** the list is fully readable with zero clicks, which is exactly what LinkedIn rewards. The "defend just one" line at the end gives people something to argue with in the comments.

## LinkedIn — Post 2 (pull out the single sharpest commandment)

> The most useful thing a mentor ever told me about being a CFO:
>
> Have a crystal-clear conviction of the long term — as long as you stay open to changing the crystal if the landscape demands it.
>
> That single sentence resolves the tension most finance leaders live inside. You're asked to be decisive and to be adaptable, and those sound like opposites. They aren't.
>
> Conviction is what stops you drifting with every quarter's noise. Adaptability is what stops conviction curdling into stubbornness. You need both, held at the same time.
>
> The failure mode on one side is a CFO who changes direction every board meeting. The failure mode on the other is a CFO defending a forecast reality has already overtaken.
>
> The discipline is knowing which one you're doing — and being honest when the crystal genuinely needs to change.
>
> One caveat my mentor added, and I'd repeat it: none of this overrides integrity. When conviction and honesty pull apart, honesty wins. Every time.
>
> (Full essay on my site — link on my profile.)

**Why this works:** takes one line from the piece and gives it room to breathe. The "decisive vs adaptable isn't a contradiction" reframe is the kind of thing senior people restack.

## LinkedIn — Post 3 (the contrarian commandment — brakes)

> Most people picture the CFO as the brakes on a fast-moving company.
>
> They're not wrong. The question is what kind of brakes.
>
> There are brakes that slow a speeding car down. And there are the brakes on a Formula 1 car — the ones that let it carry more speed into the corner, because the driver trusts they'll hold.
>
> Same component. Completely different job.
>
> The finance function that only says no is the first kind. Safe, and quietly expensive — every deal it slows, every bet it talks the room out of, is a cost that never shows up on any statement.
>
> The finance function worth having is the second kind. It protects the assets and the controls, then uses that credibility to let the business take the corner faster than it otherwise could.
>
> That shift — from slowing the car to steering it — needs the right support from the Board and the CEO. It's a lonely job without it. But it's the difference between being tolerated and being trusted.
>
> (Full essay on my site — link on my profile.)

**Why this works:** the F1-brakes image is memorable and taken straight from the source. It reframes a tired stereotype about CFOs into something aspirational, which travels well past finance.

actually asks of you"), year-end or new-financial-year reflections, results
