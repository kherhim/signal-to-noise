## LinkedIn — Post 1 (the flagship — the full list)

> FP&A teams live and breathe numbers. But numbers alone don't change minds. Stories do.
>
> Ten commandments I'd hand any FP&A professional trying to make their work matter:
>
> 1. Know your audience. A CFO wants implications. A CMO wants relevance. A board wants strategy. The same model won't speak to everyone.
> 2. Don't drown them in data. Every number must earn its place. If it doesn't change the decision, it's noise.
> 3. Begin with the "so what". Headline first, proof second. Executives don't have time for suspense.
> 4. Use visuals wisely. A good chart reveals a pattern; it doesn't decorate a slide. Avoid Frankencharts.
> 5. Build bridges, not silos. Link the financials to operations, customers and strategy.
> 6. Speak human, not jargon. If your slide needs a glossary, rewrite it.
> 7. Create tension. Show the gap between plan and reality — then the path to resolve it.
> 8. Show the before and after. Quantify the delta, not just the state.
> 9. Make time a character. Trends, seasonality and cohorts explain why, and when it changes.
> 10. End with a decision. Finish with a clear, bounded ask — not "happy to take questions".
>
> If I had to keep only one, it's the last. Your job isn't to impress. It's to influence.
>
> (Wrote the long version — with worked examples — on my site. Link's on my profile.)

**Why this works:** the whole list reads with zero clicks, which is exactly what LinkedIn rewards. The "keep only one" line hands the comments something to argue about.

## LinkedIn — Post 2 (pull out the sharpest commandment — the "so what")

> The fastest way to lose an executive room: thirty minutes of context before the ask.
>
> The single most useful discipline in FP&A is the opposite. Headline first, proof second.
>
> Here's what that looks like in a real email to a CEO. Subject line: "Recommendation: pause hiring plan; still hit FY revenue." Opening line: "We can meet the £84m target while pausing 11 non-critical hires; runway extends by 3 months. Key risks: sales ramp, seasonality." Then — and only then — the detail: ramp curves, sensitivity, the hiring map.
>
> The slide version is just as blunt. Title is the conclusion, not the topic. Left side, three bullets on why. Right side, one chart that proves it.
>
> Most FP&A packs do this backwards. They build suspense, walk the room through the model's structure, and arrive at the recommendation in the last two minutes — by which point half the room has stopped listening.
>
> Lead with the decision. Everything else is support.
>
> (Full essay, with the worked examples, on my site — link on my profile.)

**Why this works:** takes one commandment and gives it a concrete, quotable example straight from the source. The email subject line is the kind of specific that senior finance people restack.

## LinkedIn — Post 3 (the contrarian one — tension and bad news)

> Most FP&A analysts soft-pedal the bad news until Q4. That's the expensive habit.
>
> Great analyses, like great stories, have conflict. What changed? Why did we miss? What's the path back? Surfacing that tension early isn't pessimism — it's the only thing that buys the business time to act.
>
> The device that lands it is a single "red thread" slide:
>
> Expectation — "Plan: 12% growth."
> Reality — "Run-rate: 8%."
> Root causes — "conversion −1.5pp, delivery +1.2 days, returns +0.8pp."
> Resolution — "We can still reach 10% by doing A, B, C. Hitting 12% needs incremental risk D."
>
> Four lines. It frames the miss honestly, names the causes, and hands the room a decision instead of an excuse. Nobody leaves confused about what happened or what happens next.
>
> The common failure isn't getting the number wrong. It's presenting variances as fate rather than choices — and hiding the gap until it's too late to close.
>
> Tension early equals time to act. Every variance is a plot twist; treat it like one.
>
> (Full essay on my site — link on my profile.)

**Why this works:** reframes "delivering bad news" — something every finance person dreads — as a skill with a repeatable template. The red-thread structure is copy-and-keep useful.

## LinkedIn — Post 4 (build bridges — the cause-and-effect chain)

> Finance isn't a mirror. It's a map.
>
> The FP&A stories that actually move an organisation don't just report the variance — they connect it to an operational lever and a customer outcome, so three teams can rally behind one action.
>
> A real one. Customer service wait times crept up, churn ticked up, revenue dipped. The version that landed in the room:
>
> "A 40-second increase in wait time in Q2 raised churn by 0.6 percentage points and cost £380k of ARR. Hiring 8 agents pays back in 5.4 months."
>
> Finance, operations and CX aligned in one meeting — not because the analysis was clever, but because the chain of cause and effect was explicit. Operational lever, customer impact, financial result, all on one line.
>
> The quiet failure mode is presenting variances as fate. "Churn was up" is a weather report. "This lever moved churn, here's what it cost, here's the fix and its payback" is a decision.
>
> Before you present a number, ask: what operational lever explains this, what's the customer impact, and how does today's fix shape next quarter?
>
> (Full essay on my site — link on my profile.)

**Why this works:** the £380k / 5.4-month example is concrete enough to quote, and the "finance is a map, not a mirror" line is a clean, restack-friendly hook.

## Substack Notes

**Note 1** — the thesis, standalone
> FP&A teams live and breathe numbers. But numbers alone don't change minds — stories do.
>
> Ten commandments, one job each: know your audience, cut the noise, lead with the "so what", and end with an ask. Master that and your work stops being interesting and starts being useful.

**Note 2** — pull-quote (my favourite line)
> "If your story needs a legend to decode it, it's not a story — it's a riddle."
>
> The jargon test I use on every slide: if it needs a glossary, rewrite it. "Gross margin compresses 160bps due to adverse mix" becomes "we sold more low-margin SKUs; every 1% shift back to core recovers ~£220k a quarter." Same fact. One of them gets acted on.

**Note 3** — question form, invites replies
> Genuine question for FP&A folk: how do most of your packs end?
>
> Mine used to end with "happy to take questions." Now they end with a bounded ask — the exact decision, the £/KPI impact, the top two risks with owners, and a date. The difference in how a room responds is not subtle. Curious where yours land.

**Note 4** — standalone observation (data discipline)
> A 45-tab OPEX workbook became a 6-slide pack. We kept three numbers: run-rate, variance vs plan, and cash impact.
>
> The COO finally engaged — because for the first time she could see her unit's levers on one page. Every number you add is a number you're asking someone to carry. Most of them don't earn it.

**Note 5** — pull-quote (time as a character)
> "Time is the silent protagonist in FP&A."
>
> A refund spike looked alarming in April. A timeline explained it: post-promotion cohorts from February churned after the free-trial window. Overlay the cohorts, the seasonality and the shipping delays, and the board stopped chasing a red herring. Rolling 13-month charts exist so Q4/Q1 optical illusions stop steering decisions.

**Note 6** — restack + take
> (Restack Post 3 — the red-thread / tension one.)
> The reframe I keep coming back to: a variance isn't fate, it's a choice you're presenting. Expectation, reality, root causes, resolution — four lines. Surface the gap early and you've handed the room time to act instead of an autopsy.

## News-pegging (when to resurface this)

Evergreen — tie it to a moment rather than waiting for one. Best pegs: budget and
planning season ("if your FP&A pack is landing flat this cycle, here's why"),
results season ("earnings week is a storytelling test as much as a numbers one"), any
"death by PowerPoint" or "nobody reads the board pack" think-piece doing the rounds,
or the arrival of a new FP&A hire on the team. Open with the peg, then drop the list:
"Everyone's [X] this week — here are the ten rules I'd hand an FP&A team to make the
numbers actually matter."
