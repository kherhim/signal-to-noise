# Ready to post — 10 commandments for the newbie CFO

*Canonical: https://signal-to-noise.co/insights/10-commandments-newbie-cfo/*

A numbered "commandments" piece is one of the highest save-and-share formats on
LinkedIn — evergreen, scannable, and endlessly resurfaceable. This one has a
built-in audience: anyone stepping into the CFO seat for the first time, plus the
aspiring finance leaders who want to know what the job actually asks. The list
does half the work — it invites people to argue which commandment they'd add, and
to send it to the newly-promoted CFO in their network. Finished posts below, not
scaffolds. Nothing in any post body contains a link.

---

## LinkedIn — Post 1 (the flagship — the full list)

> When I first stepped into the CFO seat, I wrote down the commandments I wished someone had handed me on day one.
>
> Ten of them.
>
> 1. Help the Board set strategy — and execute it. Where to play, where to win, and, just as often, where not to play. A good strategy without an execution plan is a dream.
> 2. Remember the customer, all the time. A company exists to serve an unmet need. Forget that, and you spend your way into guessing.
> 3. Help the CEO build a strong culture. Strategy is the road. Culture is the compass.
> 4. Be the champion of change. "We've always done it this way" is where output focus quietly replaces outcome focus.
> 5. Coach and develop high performers. Learn to spot the ones who talk the talk, walk the talk — and do both.
> 6. Build your understanding of cloud and crypto. Snow melts from the edges. Learn to see around them.
> 7. Remember cash is king. Revenue is vanity, profit is sanity, cash is reality.
> 8. Understand your costs. If you can't sell a product for more than it costs to make, the unit economics never work.
> 9. Maximise return on capital employed. Units sold and market share are the wrong metric to worship.
> 10. Lead with both charisma and character — and never let the charisma mask the character.
>
> If I could only keep one on day one, it's number seven. Everything else buys you time you don't have without it.
>

**Why this works:** the full list reads with zero clicks, which is what LinkedIn rewards. The "only keep one" line at the end gives people something to argue with in the comments.

## LinkedIn — Post 2 (pull out the single sharpest commandment — cash)

> The shortest lesson I'd give any first-time CFO:
>
> Revenue is vanity. Profit is sanity. Cash is reality. Everything else is madness.
>
> You can value a company on a multiple of almost anything — revenue, EBITDA, EBIT, earnings before everything else. And for a while, it works.
>
> But depending on where a firm sits in its life-cycle, all of those measures are borrowing against the same fact: if the business doesn't eventually generate cash for the people who funded it, it goes out of business.
>
> This is the trap the numbers hide. A company can grow revenue, book profit, celebrate the multiple — and quietly run out of the one thing that keeps the lights on.
>
> The newbie CFO's job isn't to be the pessimist in the room. It's to keep the room honest about which measure is vanity, which is sanity, and which is reality.
>
> Get that hierarchy straight early, and most other financial disciplines fall into place behind it.
>
> (Full essay on my site — link on my profile.)

**Why this works:** takes one memorable line straight from the piece and gives it room to breathe. The revenue/profit/cash ladder is quotable and endlessly restack-able.

## LinkedIn — Post 3 (the contrarian commandment — champion of change)

> As a new CFO, you get told very quickly how things are done here.
>
> Because they've always been done that way.
>
> That sentence is where good finance functions go to sleep. It's the moment a team stops being outcome-focused and becomes output-focused — treading the same path with the blinders on, mistaking motion for progress.
>
> Output focus feels safe. You do the doing. The reports go out on time. Nobody complains.
>
> Outcome focus is harder. It means asking whether the doing still serves the goal at all — and being willing to tap the wider world for a better way to do it.
>
> That shift needs someone senior enough to challenge the default, and stubborn enough to keep asking. On most leadership teams, that someone is the CFO.
>
> Change is hard. It asks for brave — sometimes heart-breaking — decisions. But a newbie CFO who protects the status quo has already misread the job.
>
> (Full essay on my site — link on my profile.)

**Why this works:** reframes a tired stereotype — finance as the guardian of "how it's always been done" — into the opposite. The output-vs-outcome distinction travels well beyond finance.

## Substack Notes

**Note 1** — the origin, standalone
> When I first became a CFO, I wrote down the commandments I wished someone had handed me on day one. Ten of them.
>
> The one I'd defend first is cash. Revenue is vanity, profit is sanity, cash is reality — and every other discipline buys you time you don't have without it.

**Note 2** — pull-quote (culture line)
> "Strategy is the road. Culture is the compass."
>
> A newbie CFO learns fast that the best-laid strategy fails without a culture aligned behind it. Strategy defines the formal logic of what a company wants to do. Culture is what actually guides the doing.

**Note 3** — question form, invites replies
> Genuine question for anyone stepping into a first CFO role: if you could only master one thing in year one, what would it be?
>
> Mine was cash. Not revenue, not the multiple — the actual money in the account. Curious whether the people further along the road would say the same.

**Note 4** — standalone observation (talent)
> A blind spot nobody warns a new CFO about: it's a powerful seat, and people will say things to please you.
>
> Which is why the real skill in developing talent is telling apart three groups — those who talk the talk, those who walk the talk, and the rare few who do both. The last group becomes your leadership bench.

**Note 5** — restack + take
> (Restack Post 3 — the champion-of-change one.)
