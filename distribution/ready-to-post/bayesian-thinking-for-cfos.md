# Ready to post — Bayesian thinking for CFOs

*Canonical: https://signal-to-noise.co/insights/bayesian-thinking-for-cfos/*

This one travels because it isn't really a finance piece — it's a decision-making-under-uncertainty
framework dressed in a CFO's clothes. Operators, product people and anyone who's ever
defended a forecast past its expiry date will argue with it in the comments. The named idea
("the ledger is deterministic; the business never was") is quotable and portable. These are
finished, not scaffolds. Edit for your own ear, then post. Nothing here contains a link in the body.

---

## LinkedIn — Post 1 (the flagship — the ledger vs the business)

> A forecast is not a fact. It's an assumption in a nice suit.
>
> Most of finance is built on the opposite premise — that an answer is either right or wrong, that the same inputs produce the same outputs, that you can run an audit on certainty. And that's correct, for the ledger. Double-entry, reconciliations, controls: you cannot run an audit on vibes.
>
> But the business the ledger reports on has never worked that way. Behaviour, volatility, acquisition cost, conversion, retention, spreads, regulation — and the occasional piece of complete nonsense that turns up on a Tuesday afternoon and ruins the shape of the month.
>
> The ledger is deterministic. The business never was.
>
> Which is why the CFOs who cope best aren't the ones who sound certain. They're the ones who hold a view and let it be moved. Start with a position. Update as the evidence arrives. Try not to fall in love with the first answer.
>
> The damage rarely comes from being wrong at the outset. It comes from being slow to admit the evidence has started pointing somewhere else.
>
> Confidence is only useful when it's paired with a willingness to update quickly. Everything else is decoration.
>
> (Full essay on my site — link on my profile.)

**Why this works:** opens with a sharp, arguable claim ("assumptions in a nice suit"), not an announcement. The deterministic-ledger / probabilistic-business split is the named idea, and it's quotable. No link tax.

## LinkedIn — Post 2 (why finance finds AI unsettling)

> Here's a quieter reason some finance functions are struggling with AI than they'll admit in the town hall.
>
> Ask a model the same question twice and you may get two different answers. What comes back is a distribution, not a fact — and, unhelpfully, it rarely sounds like one.
>
> To a team raised entirely on determinism, that reads as a defect. Same inputs, same outputs — that's the whole apparatus. A tool that won't give you the same answer twice looks broken.
>
> But to anyone who thinks in Bayesian terms, it feels oddly familiar. It's how the business itself has always behaved, even when the ledger pretended otherwise. Forecasts, valuations, capital decisions — all probabilistic, all along.
>
> I suspect that's why some finance functions are adapting faster than others. The ones getting on with it already knew most of what matters was never deterministic. They're not being asked to abandon rigour. They're being asked to buy a distribution by the unit — and they'd already made peace with distributions.
>
> The ones struggling aren't behind on the technology. They're behind on the admission that certainty was decorative the whole time.
>
> (Full essay on my site — link on my profile.)

**Why this works:** reframes "AI adoption" from a tooling problem to a mindset one, which is more interesting and more shareable. "Buy a distribution by the unit" is a line people repeat.

## LinkedIn — Post 3 (the two ditches — short and punchy)

> Two ways to get uncertainty wrong, and I've watched businesses fall into both.
>
> The first ditch is paralysis. Waiting for evidence that never fully arrives. Holding the decision open until certainty shows up — and it doesn't, or at least it never has for me.
>
> The second is dogma. Treating an early thesis as destiny. A product outperforms for six weeks and it's a victory lap — never mind that six weeks is a small sample, and cohorts have a habit of reverting once you stop admiring them.
>
> Neither lasts. What lasts is the habit of disciplined revision — changing your mind for the right reasons, at the right speed.
>
> The best finance leaders I know aren't the ones who never change their minds. They're the ones who change them for the right reasons, at the right speed. That's not a personality trait. It's a skill you can practise.
>
> Worth asking which ditch you tend towards. Most of us have a favourite.
>
> (Longer version on my site — profile link.)

**Why this works:** clean two-part framework, forces self-reflection, and the "six weeks is a small sample" line is a specific, restack-able detail lifted straight from the piece.

## Substack Notes (space these over ~2 weeks)

**Note 1** — the reframe, standalone
> A forecast is not a fact. It's an assumption in a nice suit.
>
> A view of the world based on the evidence available at the time — which means if the evidence changes, the view should change. Obvious, you'd think. In most businesses it turns out to be genuinely hard. People get attached. They defend the number. They wait for more certainty, as if certainty ever arrived on schedule.

**Note 2** — the named idea (pull-quote)
> "The ledger is deterministic. The business never was."
>
> The whole apparatus of finance — double-entry, reconciliations, controls — assumes an answer is either right or wrong. That's correct, and it's the point. But it only describes the ledger. The thing the ledger reports on has always been probabilistic. Most of the discomfort in the job lives in that gap.

**Note 3** — the AI line (my favourite in the piece)
> "To a finance team raised entirely on determinism, a distribution reads as a defect. To anyone who thinks in Bayesian terms it feels oddly familiar. It's how the business itself has always behaved, even when the ledger pretended otherwise."
>
> I didn't set out to write about AI. But once you accept the business was never deterministic, "buying a distribution by the unit" stops sounding strange and starts sounding like Tuesday.

**Note 4** — question form (invites replies)
> Genuine question for finance leaders: when was the last time you changed a forecast *before* the evidence forced you to — and said so out loud?
>
> Because the damage rarely comes from being wrong at the outset. It comes from being slow to admit the evidence has started pointing somewhere else. I'd love to know whether you think that's a discipline problem or a courage one.

**Note 5** — restack + take
> (Restack Post 3 — the two ditches.)
> The reframe I keep coming back to: the best finance leaders aren't the ones who never change their minds. They're the ones who change them for the right reasons, at the right speed. Bayesian thinking just gives you permission to be provisional without being weak.

## News-pegging (when to resurface this)

Resurface any time the environment humbles a forecast: a surprise print, a
central-bank pivot, a guidance cut, a cohort that reverted, a market move nobody
modelled. Open with the news, pivot to the discipline: "Everyone's rebuilding their
model this week. The skill was never the model — it was how cleanly you update it."
Also strong alongside any "AI in finance" news cycle: pair it with the point that a
non-deterministic tool only feels like a defect if you'd forgotten the business was
never deterministic either.
