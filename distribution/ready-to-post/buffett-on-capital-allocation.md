# Ready to post — Buffett on capital allocation

*Canonical: https://signal-to-noise.co/insights/buffett-on-capital-allocation/*

This one travels on borrowed authority: the Buffett name carries reach well past
the usual finance circle, and the core claim — that capital allocation is the
whole CFO job and most functions treat it as a footnote — is contrarian enough to
draw comment. It's part of a Buffett series, so it re-posts cleanly around

## LinkedIn — Post 1 (the flagship — lead with the number on the wall)

> After ten years in the chair, a CEO will have chosen where more than 60% of the company's capital went. Almost no CFO function is built around that fact.
>
> Buffett put the number plainly in the 1987 letter:
>
> "After ten years on the job, a CEO whose company annually retains earnings equal to 10% of net worth will have been responsible for the deployment of more than 60% of all the capital at work in the business."
>
> Read that as a CFO. Sixty percent of the invested capital base, allocated by the current management team within a single tenure. Not inherited from a founder. Not handed down by a board. Chosen — one retention decision at a time, every quarter, every approval, every "let's keep the cash and see what comes up."
>
> And yet most finance functions spend their training and attention on the close, the controls, the FP&A story, the investor script — the visible job — while capital allocation gets treated as a once-a-year budgeting exercise.
>
> The accidental version of the job is the one that happens by default. If the CFO function does not own the rigour, no one else will.
>
> (Full essay — three Berkshire letters, read in sequence — on my site. Link on my profile.)

**Why this works:** opens with a concrete, surprising number, not an announcement. Uses a real Buffett quote as the payload. The "60% chosen, not inherited" reframe is the sharable turn.

## LinkedIn — Post 2 (the one-dollar test as a decision rule)

> Most internal capital cases ask the wrong question. They ask whether the project clears the hurdle rate. Buffett asks whether it clears the shareholder.
>
> The test, from the 1984 letter:
>
> "Unrestricted earnings should be retained only when there is a reasonable prospect — backed preferably by historical evidence or, when appropriate, by a thoughtful analysis of the future — that for every dollar retained by the corporation, at least one dollar of market value will be created for owners."
>
> Strip out the qualifiers. Every dollar the company keeps is a claim that the firm has a better use for it than the owner does. If you can't back that claim with evidence, the dollar belongs to the owner.
>
> Here is why it reframes the job. The hurdle rate is internal — does the project beat our cost of capital. Buffett's comparator is external — does it beat what the owner could do with the dollar if you sent it back. Both have to clear. Most cases only test the first.
>
> Capex is a one-dollar-test decision. Working capital is a one-dollar-test decision. So is the choice not to buy back stock, not to pay a special dividend, not to delever. Each is a quiet capital deployment by default.
>
> (Full essay on my site — link on my profile.)

**Why this works:** hands the reader a quotable Buffett line and a rule they can apply to Monday's approval memo. "Internal vs external comparator" is a clean, restack-able distinction.

## LinkedIn — Post 3 (five paths, not one decision)

> Ask a CFO how they allocate capital and most describe one or two moves they're fluent in. Buffett treats it as a taxonomy of five — and weighs every retained dollar against all of them.
>
> From the 2014 letter, on how Berkshire actually deploys capital:
>
> "Charlie and I hope to build Berkshire's per-share intrinsic value by (1) constantly improving the basic earning power of our many subsidiaries; (2) further increasing their earnings through bolt-on acquisitions; (3) benefiting from the growth of our investees; (4) repurchasing Berkshire shares when they are available at a meaningful discount from intrinsic value; and (5) making an occasional large acquisition."
>
> It looks unremarkable until you see what it is. Not five strategies. A taxonomy. Every retained dollar travels down exactly one of the five — and the discipline is to weigh it against the other four, every time.
>
> Most functions don't. A growth-stage company is fluent in (1), maybe (2), and treats buybacks as a once-a-year IR conversation. A mature one defaults to (1) because that's what the operating P&L absorbs. In each case the institution has quietly pre-decided where dollars go, and the retention test stops getting asked.
>
> The practical move: in every retention case, state which path the dollar travels, why that path beats the other four for this specific dollar, and the test that will tell you in three years whether you were right.
>
> (Full essay on my site — link on my profile.)

**Why this works:** turns an abstract Buffett list into a concrete process the reader can adopt. The "you've pre-decided, so you stopped asking" line names a failure people recognise in themselves.

## Substack Notes (space these out)

**Note 1** — the number on the wall
> "After ten years on the job, a CEO whose company annually retains earnings equal to 10% of net worth will have been responsible for the deployment of more than 60% of all the capital at work in the business." — Buffett, 1987 letter.
>
> Sixty percent of the invested base, chosen within one tenure — one retention decision at a time. That sentence is the entire CFO job, and almost no function is organised around it.

**Note 2** — the one-dollar test, standalone
> "For every dollar retained by the corporation, at least one dollar of market value will be created for owners." — Buffett, 1984 letter.
>
> Every dollar you keep is a claim that the firm has a better use for it than the shareholder does. The hurdle rate tests whether the project beats your cost of capital. This tests whether it beats the owner. Different question.

**Note 3** — the accidental allocator
> "Once they become CEOs, they face new responsibilities. They now must make capital allocation decisions, a critical job that they may have never tackled and that is not easily mastered." — Buffett, 1987 letter.
>
> The accidental nature of it isn't a CEO problem. It's a finance problem. If the CFO function doesn't keep the framework, the accidental version of the job is the one that runs.

**Note 4** — question form (invites replies)
> Genuine question for finance leaders: of the five paths Buffett names — subsidiary earning power, bolt-ons, investee growth, buybacks, a large acquisition — how many has your function actually rehearsed?
>
> The CFO who has never run a real buyback evaluation won't run a good one under pressure. Which one is your under-rehearsed muscle?

**Note 5** — the reframe I keep returning to
> The vocabulary of finance has changed in forty years. The discipline hasn't. The 1984 letter is forty years old, the 1987 nearly forty, the 2014 more than ten — and every one of them is describing the same job most functions still treat as a quarterly artefact.
>
> AI has lowered the cost of the visible parts of finance. It has not lowered the cost of judgement. That's the part still worth owning.
