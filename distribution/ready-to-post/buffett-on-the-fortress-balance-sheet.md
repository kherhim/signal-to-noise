## LinkedIn — Post 1 (the flagship — lead with the contrarian claim)

> Most CFOs are trained to make the balance sheet efficient. Buffett spent his career arguing the opposite.
>
> The standard playbook is familiar: minimise idle cash, lever up toward the efficient frontier, lift return on equity by financing the business as leanly as the covenants allow. In that view a fat balance sheet is the mark of a lazy CFO.
>
> Buffett's view is that the balance sheet's first job is not to be efficient. It is to be unkillable.
>
> His own words, from the 2008 letter, written into the teeth of the crisis:
>
> "I have pledged — to you, the rating agencies and myself — to always run Berkshire with more than ample cash. We never want to count on the kindness of strangers in order to meet tomorrow's obligations. When forced to choose, I will not trade even a night's sleep for the chance of extra profits."
>
> Read that last line as a CFO. He is explicitly declining a return — accepting a permanently lower ROE — in exchange for never being a forced seller.
>
> And here is the part a risk-averse reading misses. The reward for building it that way is not safety. It is the freedom to act when everyone around you has lost the ability to.
>
> In a capital cycle running this hot, that is the muscle most worth keeping.
>
> (Full essay — three Berkshire letters, read in sequence — on my site. Link on my profile.)

**Why this works:** opens with the argument someone wants to fight, not an announcement. Uses a real Buffett quote as the payload. Ends by recasting caution as offence, which is the surprising turn.

## LinkedIn — Post 2 (judge leverage by the zero)

> The single sentence that should end most "optimal capital structure" debates comes from Buffett's 2010 letter:
>
> "Any series of positive numbers, however impressive the numbers may be, evaporates when multiplied by a single zero. History tells us that leverage all too often produces zeroes, even when it is employed by very smart people."
>
> Here is why that reframes everything.
>
> WACC optimisation is an argument about the average case — finance the business so the blended cost of capital is lowest across normal conditions. Buffett's point is that leverage is not judged by the average. It is judged by the zero.
>
> A capital structure tuned for the expected path can be ended outright by a single realised tail. And the tail does not care how good the preceding numbers were.
>
> The mechanism is mundane. Companies with large debts assume those obligations can be refinanced as they mature. Usually true. But occasionally — company-specific trouble, or a worldwide credit shortage — maturities have to be met by payment. For that, only cash will do the job.
>
> So run one exercise. Take the refinancing you are quietly assuming in your plan. Ask what happens if that window is shut for twelve months. If the honest answer is "we don't survive," the structure is wrong — whatever it does to ROE in the years the window stays open.
>
> Optimising for the nineteen good quarters is not a strategy if the twentieth one is fatal.
>
> (Full essay on my site — link on my profile.)

**Why this works:** hands the reader a quotable Buffett line and a concrete test they can run on Monday. The "nineteen good quarters" framing is restack-able on its own.

## LinkedIn — Post 3 (the fortress is offence)

> A fortress balance sheet gets read as the timid choice. Buffett's record says the opposite.
>
> In September 2008, many long-prosperous companies suddenly wondered whether their cheques would bounce. Their financial oxygen disappeared overnight. Buffett's line from the 2014 letter:
>
> "Cash, though, is to a business as oxygen is to an individual: never thought about when it is present, the only thing in mind when it is absent."
>
> At Berkshire, the breathing went uninterrupted. In a three-week period spanning late September and early October, it supplied $15.6 billion of fresh money to American businesses.
>
> The Goldman Sachs and General Electric deals came with a substantial equity participation as a bonus — available on those terms for one reason. Berkshire could write the cheque when almost no one else could.
>
> The cash that looked like a drag on ROE in the calm years is exactly what let it act decisively in the one year that paid.
>
> For a 2026 CFO deciding how lean to run into the AI-capex cycle, that reframes the question. Not only "can we survive a downturn." Rather: when the cycle breaks and assets are cheap, do we want to be the buyer or the seller?
>
> A balance sheet optimised for normal conditions quietly chooses seller — because in the moment that matters, it will be raising cash, not deploying it.
>
> (Full essay on my site — link on my profile.)

**Why this works:** flips a defensive topic into an aggressive one, anchored by the $15.6bn figure and a real quote. The buyer-or-seller question is the sharable hook.
