# Ready to post — Cashing in on cheers: a CFO's guide to making sports sponsorships pay

*Canonical: https://signal-to-noise.co/insights/cashing-cheers-cfos-guide-making-sports-sponsorships-pay/*

## LinkedIn — Post 1 (the flagship — measurement, not spend)

> A sports sponsorship is not the hard decision. Measuring it is.
>
> Sanctioning the budget is easy. The real job for a CFO is making sure that spend becomes a strategic investment with tangible returns — not just a logo on a shirt.
>
> For too long, sponsorships have been judged on visibility and reach. Important, but nowhere near the full picture. They tell you the message travelled. They don't tell you whether it moved anything.
>
> The correction is to decide, before the money leaves, what a return actually looks like. Define quantifiable objectives that align to the broader business goals. Agree the financial metrics — direct sales growth and conversion, and the indirect ones: brand value, customer lifetime value, market share. Agree the qualitative measures too, and then hold both honestly.
>
> That last part is where it usually breaks. The finance folks carry a long bias towards the financial metrics; the marketing folks do exactly the same for the qualitative stuff. A framework only works when it forces both sides to the same table before the outlay.
>
> Passion is real. The question is whether you've built the means to price it.
>
> (Full six-step framework on my site — link on my profile.)

**Why this works:** opens with a claim that reframes the CFO's job, not an announcement. Names the finance-vs-marketing bias everyone recognises. Ends on a question, no link tax.

## LinkedIn — Post 2 (the pitfall — gut instinct)

> Here's how most sponsorship decisions actually get made: gut instinct, or competitive pressure because a rival got there first.
>
> And doing it that way often destroys more value than it creates for the sponsoring firm.
>
> The pull is understandable. Sport offers something other media can't fake — the live, unscripted moment, the association with teamwork, determination and excellence, a direct line to the heart of consumer passion. Nike and global football. Red Bull and extreme sports. When the alignment is right, these aren't transactions; they're brand-building tools.
>
> But alignment isn't an accident, and it isn't a feeling. The common trap is fixating on immediate sales uplift while ignoring the slower effects on brand equity and customer loyalty. The second trap is data collection too thin to capture what a sponsorship actually did.
>
> Getting it right demands breaking the silos — finance and marketing aligned on the same objectives, using real analytics and market research rather than the loudest voice in the room.
>
> Enthusiasm is not a business case. Build the case first, then feel the enthusiasm.
>
> (The full guide is on my site — profile link.)

**Why this works:** names an uncomfortable habit the target reader has seen up close. The "destroys more value than it creates" line is quotable and lands as a warning, not a lecture.

## LinkedIn — Post 3 (the short, punchy one — before the outlay)

> Three things every CFO should agree before a single pound of sponsorship money is committed:
>
> **The objectives** — quantifiable, and tied to the business, not the marketing plan.
>
> **The financial metrics** — direct sales and conversion, plus the indirect ones: brand value, customer lifetime value, market share.
>
> **The qualitative method** — how you'll assess loyalty, brand alignment and sentiment, agreed up front so it isn't argued about later.
>
> Decide these after the deal is signed and you're not measuring a return. You're rationalising a spend.
>
> The value of sport unfolds over time. So does the cost of measuring it badly.
>
> (Longer version on my site — profile link.)

**Why this works:** clean three-part checklist, highly restack-able, and the "rationalising a spend" turn forces a rethink.

## Substack Notes (space these over ~2 weeks)

**Note 1** — the reframe, standalone
> Sports sponsorships have long been judged on visibility and reach. Useful numbers — and nowhere near enough.
>
> They tell you the message travelled. They don't tell you it changed a sale, a loyalty, or a share of market. The job is to turn an expenditure into an investment, and that starts with deciding what a return looks like before the money moves.

**Note 2** — the bias nobody admits
> The biggest obstacle to measuring sponsorship ROI isn't the data. It's the people reading it.
>
> Finance folks carry a long bias towards the financial metrics. Marketing folks do the same for the qualitative stuff. A framework earns its keep only when it drags both to the same table — before the outlay, not after.

**Note 3** — question form, invites replies
> Genuine question for anyone who's signed off a sponsorship: was the return defined before the money left, or reverse-engineered after the season ended?
>
> Because visibility and reach are easy to report. Sales growth, brand equity, customer lifetime value, opportunity cost — that's the harder ledger, and it has to be agreed up front.

**Note 4** — standalone observation
> Most sponsorship calls are made on gut instinct or competitive pressure — a rival got there, so we must too.
>
> And more often than people admit, that destroys more value than it creates. Sport's authenticity is real. So is the cost of buying into it without a way to price the return.

**Note 5** — restack + one line
> (Restack Post 1 here.)
> Passion, engagement and loyalty are the easy parts of a sponsorship to feel. The whole discipline is translating them into financial returns you can actually stand behind.
