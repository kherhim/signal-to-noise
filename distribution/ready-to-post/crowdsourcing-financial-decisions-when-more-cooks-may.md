# Ready to post — Crowdsourcing financial decisions: when more cooks may not spoil the broth

*Canonical: https://signal-to-noise.co/insights/crowdsourcing-financial-decisions-when-more-cooks-may/*

This one travels because it picks a fight with a reflex every finance leader shares: that the sharpest individual in the room should own the call. The claim — that a well-designed crowd can out-forecast your best analyst — is contrarian enough to argue about, and the design conditions give commenters something concrete to disagree over. Debate-friendly by construction. Finished posts below. Nothing here contains a link in the body.

---

## LinkedIn — Post 1 (the flagship — lead with the contrarian claim)

> Under the right conditions, a group's collective insight can beat the smartest individual in it.
>
> That includes your best forecaster.
>
> It's the core argument of James Surowiecki's "The Wisdom of Crowds," and it lands harder in finance than most places — because we still default to a few experts or insiders on the decisions that matter most.
>
> A tech giant tested it. It crowdsourced revenue forecasting by anonymously pooling predictions from across the business — not just finance. The result was greater accuracy, plus insights from corners of the organisation nobody thought to ask.
>
> But the wisdom only shows up under conditions. Surowiecki names three: diversity of opinion, independence, decentralisation. Add a proper mechanism to aggregate the views into one decision.
>
> Miss those and you don't get wisdom. You get a herd.
>
> So the CFO question isn't "who's my smartest person on this." It's "have I built a crowd diverse and independent enough to be worth listening to."
>
> That's a design problem, not a headcount one.
>
> (I wrote the long version — the conditions, the examples, where it breaks — on my site. Link's on my profile.)

**Why this works:** opens with a claim that challenges the reader's own instinct, names the source idea, and turns the ending into a reframe rather than an ask. The "beat your best forecaster" line is what gets argued in the comments.

## LinkedIn — Post 2 (the design-conditions angle)

> Most crowdsourced decisions fail for the same reason: the crowd was never designed.
>
> "Wisdom of crowds" is not a headcount. Throw a question at a big enough group and you often get the average of everyone's bias, not insight.
>
> The conditions are what do the work. For a finance crowd, three of them matter:
>
> Diverse — pull from different parts of the business, not one function.
>
> Independent — they don't all report to the same person, so nobody's view quietly anchors everyone else's.
>
> Skin in the game — a stake in the outcome, tied to the same operating metric, to blunt groupthink.
>
> Then the failure modes. Herd mentality, where independence collapses. And information cascades, where a few dominant voices skew the whole result.
>
> The fixes are mechanical, not motivational: anonymise contributions, weight opinions by expertise or track record, and frame the question so it draws out something actionable.
>
> A multinational opened its strategic planning to a wider group through a digital platform — employees, stakeholders, selected external experts. What it got back was a more flexible investment strategy that could adapt as markets moved.
>
> The crowd was useful because it was built to be.
>
> (Full breakdown on my site — link on my profile.)

**Why this works:** takes the buzzword and makes it operational. The three conditions plus two failure modes give the reader a checklist they can apply on Monday, which is what gets it saved and shared.

## LinkedIn — Post 3 (the short, punchy one)

> A crowd that all reports to you is not a crowd.
>
> It's you, echoed back.
>
> The whole value of collective decision-making comes from independence — views that don't anchor on each other. The moment everyone's looking sideways to see what the senior person thinks, the wisdom is gone and you're left with the average of everyone's caution.
>
> This is why the strongest crowdsourced forecasts are anonymous. Not for comfort. To protect independence.
>
> Worth asking, before your next big call: is the group around this decision genuinely independent — or just a well-mannered reflection of the most senior voice in the room?
>
> (Longer version on my site — profile link.)

**Why this works:** one sharp line up top, a single idea (independence) carried cleanly through, and a self-audit question at the end. Restack-able because it names a discomfort most teams recognise.

## Substack Notes (space these over ~2 weeks)

**Note 1** — the core claim, standalone
> "Under the right conditions, the collective insight of a group can surpass even the smartest individual within it."
>
> That's the sentence from Surowiecki that finance leaders skip past too quickly. It includes your best forecaster. The catch is the phrase "under the right conditions" — diversity, independence, decentralisation, and a way to aggregate the views. Miss those and the crowd is just a herd.

**Note 2** — the example
> A tech giant crowdsourced its revenue forecasting — anonymously pooling predictions from across the whole business, not only finance.
>
> Two things came out of it: better accuracy, and insights from parts of the organisation nobody had thought to ask. That second one is the part people underrate. Diversity doesn't just refine the answer; it surfaces questions you weren't asking.

**Note 3** — the design point
> A crowd is not a headcount. It's a set of conditions.
>
> For a finance crowd: diverse (different parts of the business), independent (they don't all report to the same person), and with a genuine stake in the outcome. Get those and you get wisdom. Get them wrong and you get groupthink with more people in the room.

**Note 4** — question form, invites replies
> Genuine question for anyone who's tried crowdsourcing a forecast: did you protect the independence of the inputs — or did the first few confident voices quietly set the anchor for everyone after?
>
> Surowiecki calls that second thing an information cascade. I suspect it's the reason most attempts quietly disappoint. Curious whether that matches your experience.

**Note 5** — restack + take
> (Restack Post 3 — the independence one.)
> The reframe I keep returning to: a crowd that all reports to you isn't a crowd, it's you echoed back. Anonymity in these processes isn't about comfort. It's the mechanism that keeps the inputs independent enough to be worth aggregating.
