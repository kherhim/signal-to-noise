## LinkedIn — Post 1 (the flagship — the core reframe)

> Presenting numbers is not storytelling.
>
> A financial report that only presents numbers informs. A financial story weaves those numbers into a narrative that drives decisions and inspires action.
>
> The difference comes down to a handful of habits most finance teams already half-know.
>
> Know your audience. Executives want strategic implications; analysts want the granular detail. Tailor the message, and highlight only the data points that are relevant to the person in the room — ROI and growth for investors, cost efficiencies for operations.
>
> Set the stage with context. A number on its own means nothing. Compare it against historical performance and industry benchmarks so the trend, and the deviation, are visible.
>
> Simplify. Stick to the KPIs that matter — too many metrics dilute the message. Use visuals to make complex data digestible, and design the report to guide the eye through the story in a logical sequence.
>
> Then land the insight. Not "here is the data," but "here is what you should do with it."
>
> The goal was never to present. It's to inform, engage, and move people to a decision.
>
> (I wrote the full fifteen-point cheat sheet on my site. Link's on my profile.)

**Why this works:** opens with a flat, contrarian claim ("presenting numbers is not storytelling"), then delivers genuine utility in the body. Save-worthy, which drives shares over comments.

## LinkedIn — Post 2 (the trust angle — data integrity)

> Your story is only as good as the assumptions and the data behind it.
>
> This is the part of financial storytelling that gets skipped, because it's the least glamorous. But it's the part that decides whether anyone acts on what you present.
>
> Three habits hold it together.
>
> Cite your sources. Credibility isn't assumed; it's shown.
>
> Be transparent about limitations and assumptions. Naming what you don't know builds trust and prepares your audience for an informed decision — it doesn't weaken the case, it strengthens it.
>
> Use consistent metrics and definitions across every report. Different parts of the company keep their own data sources and mark their own homework. See through that process. Inconsistent definitions don't just cause confusion; they quietly destroy comparability.
>
> There's a discipline point buried in the analytics too: it's easy to data-dump, harder to segment intelligently. And when you forecast, incorporate seasonality and trends rather than assuming a straight line.
>
> Transparency isn't a compliance footnote. It's the thing that makes the story usable.
>
> (Full cheat sheet — fifteen points — on my site. Link on my profile.)

**Why this works:** takes the least-discussed section and treats it as the load-bearing one. The "marks their own homework" line is a scroll-stopper for anyone who's chased a reconciliation.

## LinkedIn — Post 3 (the short, framework one)

> Every good financial story has three parts.
>
> **The context** — where we were, and against what. Historical comparison and industry benchmarks, so the number has a backdrop.
>
> **The journey** — the data and the analysis. The problem, examined honestly.
>
> **The resolution** — the strategic insight, and the action it implies.
>
> Beginning, middle, end. Same structure the best reports have always used, and the same one most decks quietly skip — they open with the data and never reach the resolution.
>
> Start with the context. Delve into the data. Conclude with what to do next.
>
> Worth asking whether your last board pack actually had all three.
>
> (Longer version — fifteen points — on my site. Profile link.)

**Why this works:** clean three-part framework, highly restack-able, and ends on a question that forces the reader to audit their own work.
