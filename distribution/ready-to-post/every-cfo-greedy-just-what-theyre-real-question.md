# Ready to post — Every CFO is greedy

*Canonical: https://signal-to-noise.co/insights/every-cfo-greedy-just-what-theyre-real-question/*

This one leads with a deliberately provocative claim — "every CFO is greedy" — that

## LinkedIn — Post 1 (the flagship — lead with the claim)

> Every CFO is greedy. The only question that matters is what for.
>
> Short-term greed protects this year's EBITDA at all costs. It smooths volatility. It delays the investments that make the numbers look messy. And it feels responsible — you're keeping the ship steady, reducing immediate risk, handing the board a clean story to tell.
>
> I get it. I've been there.
>
> But here's what nobody says out loud: that discipline, applied consistently, quietly makes a company more fragile. You're not cutting fat. You're cutting muscle. And you won't know it until you need to move fast and can't.
>
> Long-term greed is harder. It means investing ahead of revenue, accepting a period of margin compression, building capability before it's urgent. Designing incentives around three-year returns instead of annual targets. Thinking in capital cycles, not reporting cycles.
>
> It's lonelier, because you're making the case for decisions whose payoff won't show up on anyone's scorecard this year.
>
> So the honest question on every capital allocation call is the same one: am I optimising the period, or the franchise?
>
> (The full essay is on my site — link on my profile.)

**Why this works:** "Every CFO is greedy" is a claim the target reader will stop to argue with, then the "what for" turn reframes the fight into their own dilemma. No announcement, no link tax.

## LinkedIn — Post 2 (the fragility angle)

> The most dangerous phrase in finance is "we're just being disciplined."
>
> Because there's a kind of discipline that looks exactly like prudence and is quietly the opposite of it.
>
> You protect the quarter. You smooth the volatility. You delay the investment that would have made this year's numbers look messy. Every one of those calls feels responsible in the moment. Steady ship. Lower risk. Clean story for the board.
>
> Do it consistently and you don't end up safer. You end up more fragile. You're not cutting fat — you're cutting muscle. And you find out which one it was at the worst possible time: the moment you need to move fast and can't.
>
> This is the uncomfortable part. Most companies don't struggle because their people aren't smart enough. They struggle because the incentives pull people toward the wrong time horizon. Short-term greed feels safe. Long-term greed feels risky.
>
> But compounding only works if you stay in the game long enough for it to matter.
>
> A CFO's job isn't to protect earnings. It's to protect the company's ability to earn. That's a meaningfully different mandate.
>
> (Full breakdown on my site — profile link.)

**Why this works:** "cutting muscle, not fat" is a vivid, restackable line, and reframing prudence itself as a hidden risk is a claim finance leaders will want to push back on in the comments.

## LinkedIn — Post 3 (the behavioural-engineering reframe)

> Your KPIs are not a measurement system. They're a form of behavioural engineering.
>
> CFOs sit at the centre of something we rarely name out loud. We decide what gets measured, what gets rewarded, what gets capital, and what gets cut. Those aren't just financial decisions. They're cultural ones.
>
> Compensation structures shape behaviour over time. The metrics you choose teach people what to optimise for. Capital allocation, at its core, is your company's values expressed in numbers.
>
> Which means the question isn't whether your people are talented. It's whether the incentives you built are pulling them toward the right time horizon — or quietly rewarding them for optimising the period at the expense of the franchise.
>
> Value doesn't come from optics. It's endurance that gets rewarded. And endurance only compounds if the scorecard is built for it.
>
> So it's worth asking, honestly: are the incentives in your organisation built for 12 months, or for something that actually compounds?
>
> (Longer version on my site — link on my profile.)

**Why this works:** "your KPIs are behavioural engineering" reframes something every finance leader owns as a lever they may be pulling badly. Direct, specific, and it ends on a question aimed straight at the reader's own numbers.

## News-pegging (when to resurface this)

Resurface on any peg that puts short-termism back in the headlines: an activist
pushing for a buyback, a company cutting R&D or capex to defend a quarter, an
earnings-beat-at-all-costs story, a layoffs-to-protect-margin announcement, or
the annual bonus/comp-design cycle. Open with the news, pivot to the frame:
"Everyone's calling this discipline. Ask whether it's cutting fat — or muscle."
