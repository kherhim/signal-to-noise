# Ready to post — Evolving financial analytics: beyond traditional statements

*Canonical: https://signal-to-noise.co/insights/evolving-financial-analytics-navigating-beyond-traditional/*

This one travels because it takes on a topic every finance leader defends by reflex — the
financial statement — and argues its role has to evolve. It's forward-looking and AI-adjacent
(alternative data, real-time analytics, crypto, unregulated sectors) without leaving the CFO's
chair. The hooks are concrete and dated: SVB signed off two weeks before seizure, intangibles
at 90% of S&P 500 value. Finished posts below. Nothing here contains a link in the body.

---

## LinkedIn — Post 1 (the flagship — lead with the SVB dates)

> KPMG signed a clean audit opinion on Silicon Valley Bank on 24 February 2023.
>
> Two weeks later, regulators seized the bank.
>
> Signature Bank's audit opinion was dated 1 March 2023. Eleven days before it was seized too.
>
> A financial statement is a static snapshot of the past. In a world where market dynamics move this fast, the past is not the same thing as the present — and it is certainly not the future.
>
> SVB failed on a bank run on 10 March, within a month of signing its statements. The disclosure that would have told you why — standby liquidity facilities — sits hidden in the notes. A few paragraphs. At least among FTSE firms, executive compensation gets pages.
>
> That is the mismatch. We spend our disclosure budget on what is easy to report, not on what is most likely to bring the company down.
>
> Statements are not wrong. They are just not enough on their own.
>
> (I wrote the long version — eight ways the statement is quietly failing us — on my site. Link's on my profile.)

**Why this works:** two dated, verifiable facts a CFO cannot scroll past, then the turn from "audit" to "the thing that actually killed it was buried in the notes." Names the mismatch. No link tax.

## LinkedIn — Post 2 (the intangibles number)

> In 1975, intangible assets were about 17% of the value of the S&P 500.
>
> By 2022, around 90%.
>
> Brand reputation, intellectual property, human capital — the things that now carry most of a company's worth are precisely the things financial statements capture worst.
>
> We built the whole apparatus of financial analysis around what is easy to count: assets you can touch, cash you can trace. That apparatus was fit for a world where those things were most of the value. That world is gone.
>
> Which leaves an uncomfortable question for anyone signing off on a valuation. Do analysts and shareholders actually have enough to assign a value to these intangible assets? Or are we marking the 10% we can measure precisely and waving a hand at the 90% we cannot?
>
> A knowledge-based economy needs a knowledge-based way of reading a company. The balance sheet, on its own, is not it.
>
> (Full essay on my site — link on my profile.)

**Why this works:** one 47-year comparison that reframes the entire discipline. The 17%-to-90% swing is genuinely unsettling to a finance leader, and the closing question forces a reckoning rather than a nod.

## LinkedIn — Post 3 (the alternative-data reframe — brand "signal, not noise")

> In the early 2010s, Tesla's financial statements described a high-risk company.
>
> The pre-order waitlists described a different one. So did the social media sentiment. So did an external read of how fast the supercharger network was expanding.
>
> Same company. Two completely different pictures. Only one of them was in the accounts.
>
> Alternative data — customer reviews, sentiment analysis, web traffic — now offers a real-time read on performance and prospects that a quarterly statement structurally cannot. FedEx and DHL learn more from live delivery tracking and fleet efficiency than any income statement shows; they operate where a delay has immediate financial consequences.
>
> The statement is the lagging record. Increasingly, the signal arrives somewhere else first.
>
> This is not an argument for abandoning the statements. It is an argument for stewards who read both — the quantitative and the qualitative — instead of mistaking the one they can audit for the whole truth.
>
> (Longer version on my site — profile link.)

**Why this works:** the Tesla split-screen is a clean, memorable device, and "the signal arrives somewhere else first" lands the brand's whole premise. Provocative without being unmoored from the source.
