# Ready to post — Freud, finance & folly

*Canonical: https://signal-to-noise.co/insights/freud-finance-folly-cfos-perspective/*

This one travels on an unusual pairing: Freud on the CFO's desk. Behavioural
finance is a familiar topic, but reading the id, ego and superego onto capital
allocation is a fresh, slightly provocative angle — the kind of framing a
finance reader hasn't seen before and will want to argue about. Lead with the
psychology, land on the discipline. Finished posts below. No link in any body.

## LinkedIn — Post 1 (the flagship — the three-part mind)

> Finance looks like a world of numbers, graphs and cold, hard data.
>
> Behind the spreadsheets sits a very human sea of emotions, desires and psychological complexity. And Freud, of all people, maps it better than most risk frameworks.
>
> His model of the mind has three parts, and every one of them shows up in the finance function.
>
> The id runs on the pleasure principle — immediate gratification. It's the investor chasing quick returns without due diligence. It's the CFO making a hasty acquisition.
>
> The ego runs on the reality principle. It balances that impulse with careful analysis and risk assessment before capital moves.
>
> The superego holds the moral standards — the ethical line, doing what's right, not just what pays this quarter.
>
> Folly, in finance, is almost always the id overriding the ego. The dot-com bubble was that. The subprime crisis was that. Irrational exuberance guiding decisions that rational analysis would have stopped.
>
> The CFO's real job isn't to silence any one voice. It's to keep the three in balance — profit, rationality, ethics — because that balance is what sustainable financial success actually runs on.
>
> Worth asking which of the three has been driving your last few big decisions.
>
> (Full essay — Freud on capital allocation — on my site. Link on my profile.)

**Why this works:** the id/ego/superego map is an unexpected lens for a finance reader, and the dot-com/subprime line grounds an abstract idea in decisions they recognise. Ends on a question.

## LinkedIn — Post 2 (the unconscious biases angle)

> Most bad financial decisions aren't made in the numbers. They're made below them.
>
> Freud's insight that matters most for a CFO is the unconscious mind — the thoughts and desires sitting outside conscious awareness. That's where the biases live.
>
> Two of them run quietly through most finance functions.
>
> Confirmation bias: the pull to seek out information that confirms what you already believe, and to wave away the evidence that contradicts it.
>
> Overconfidence bias: the illusion of control, and the overestimation of your own ability to predict financial outcomes.
>
> Neither announces itself. Both feel like judgement. That's exactly why they're dangerous — they wear the costume of rational analysis.
>
> Recognising them, in yourself and in your team, is the first step to blunting their effect on strategy. Not eliminating them — that isn't on offer — but seeing them early enough to check the work.
>
> Which is why the most useful thing a finance leader can build isn't a better model. It's a culture of reflection and open debate, where assumptions get challenged and power circles get defied.
>
> (The full breakdown is on my site — profile link.)

**Why this works:** names two biases the reader has felt but rarely labels, then turns the fix into a leadership move, not a spreadsheet one. Specific and self-implicating.

## LinkedIn — Post 3 (the short, punchy one)

> A short checklist I keep for big financial decisions, borrowed from Freud.
>
> Before capital moves, three questions:
>
> Is this the id talking? Immediate gratification — the quick return, the hasty acquisition — dressed up as opportunity.
>
> Has the ego done its work? The careful analysis and risk assessment that should sit between the impulse and the decision.
>
> Would the superego sign it off? Not just profitable this quarter, but right — over the long term, and for the wider impact.
>
> Folly is what happens when the first voice drowns out the other two.
>
> Most finance functions are very good at the ego and almost silent on the other two. Worth knowing which voice you've actually been training to lead.
>
> (Longer version on my site — profile link.)

**Why this works:** turns the essay's framework into a reusable three-question checklist — clean, restack-able, and it forces a moment of self-audit.
