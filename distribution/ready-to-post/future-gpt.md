## LinkedIn — Post 1 (the flagship — the "chat to co-pilot" thesis)

> The chat box is the least interesting thing AI will do for your finance function.
>
> Right now most of us meet these models the same way: a blank window, a prompt, an answer we copy out and paste somewhere useful. Useful — but manual. Still noise.
>
> The picture worth planning for is different. Not chat. Co-pilots. Sitting inside the systems finance already runs on — ERP and EPM, CRM, BI, procurement. Surfacing scenario analysis without being asked. Writing the narrative summary under the numbers. Flagging the risk before you'd gone looking for it. Suggesting the next best action.
>
> Less noise. More signal. Better decisions. That's the shift — from a thing you talk to, to a thing that works alongside the close, the forecast, the board pack.
>
> But here's the part a CFO can't skip. These models stay unpredictable in exactly the situations finance cares about most — the complex, the nuanced, the genuinely ambiguous. They will draft beautifully and be confidently wrong.
>
> So the co-pilot doesn't remove the judgement. It moves it. Your job stops being production and becomes direction and oversight.
>
> Plan for the co-pilot, not the chat box. And build the checking in from day one.
>

## LinkedIn — Post 2 (the jobs reframe)

> "AI will take the finance jobs" is the wrong sentence. Here's the more useful one.
>
> The automation of language-based work is real. Drafting reports, first-pass customer replies, routine analysis — a lot of that is going to be done faster and cheaper by a model than by a person. Pretending otherwise helps no one.
>
> But the same shift that removes tasks creates a category of work that barely existed a few years ago: managing the AI. Directing it. Reviewing what it produces. Owning the outcome when it's wrong. Someone has to design the guardrails, decide what the model is allowed to touch, and check the output before it reaches a board or a regulator.
>
> That's not a footnote. In a finance function it's close to the whole game. The efficiency is easy to capture and easy to over-trust. The oversight is the hard, human part — and it's where the roles are moving.
>
> So the honest framing isn't displacement or safety. It's a rebalance. Fewer hours producing the first draft. More hours deciding whether the draft can be trusted.
>
> The teams that plan for that rebalance now will be the ones still standing in charge of it later.
>
> (Full essay on my site — link on my profile.)

**Why this works:** takes the tired "AI takes jobs" debate and reframes it as a rebalance the reader can act on — grounded in the essay's point that new roles form around AI management and oversight.

## LinkedIn — Post 3 (the governance angle — most pointed)

> Two questions decide whether AI helps or hurts your finance function. Most teams only ask the first.
>
> The first is easy, and everyone asks it: what can it do? Automate the reporting, speed up the analysis, draft the customer response, scan for fraud. The list is long and genuinely impressive.
>
> The second is the one that actually protects you: what happens when it's wrong? Because these models don't fail loudly. They fail fluently. The output that's biased, the summary that's subtly off, the confident answer to a nuanced question it didn't really understand — it all arrives looking exactly as polished as the correct output.
>
> For most industries that's a quality issue. In finance it's a control issue. Feed unchecked model output into market analysis, or personalised advice, or anything a regulator can read, and the efficiency you gained is dwarfed by the exposure you took on.
>
> So the discipline isn't clever prompting. It's guardrails — what the model is allowed to touch, how its output gets checked, who owns the answer. Boring, unglamorous, and the whole point.
>
> Adopt the capability. Govern the failure mode. Do both, or don't do it.
>
> (Full essay on my site — link on my profile.)

**Why this works:** "they fail fluently" is a sticky, quotable line, and the quality-issue-vs-control-issue distinction gives a finance reader a frame they'll repeat in their own meetings.
