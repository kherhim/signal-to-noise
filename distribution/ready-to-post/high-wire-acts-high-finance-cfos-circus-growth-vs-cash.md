# Ready to post — High-wire acts in high finance

*Canonical: https://signal-to-noise.co/insights/high-wire-acts-high-finance-cfos-circus-growth-vs-cash/*

Growth versus cash flow is a perennial finance tension, which is exactly why this
one travels. It doesn't need a fresh peg to land, but it re-posts cleanly on any
funding round, profitability warning, or "growth at all costs" headline. Each of
the four trade-offs in the essay carries a concrete worked example a CFO can argue
with. Finished posts below — nothing in the body contains a link.

---

## LinkedIn — Post 1 (the flagship — lead with the worked example)

> A midsize SaaS firm has $5m in cash and a strategically sound product bet in front of it.
>
> The bet costs $2m upfront. It returns nothing for at least 18 months post-launch. Meanwhile the firm carries $500k of monthly recurring OpEx and a quarterly tax bill of roughly $250k.
>
> Fund it, and cash reserves drop to $3m the day you sign.
>
> That is the whole job in one number. The investment case is good. The liquidity risk is real. Both are true at once.
>
> This is the tension I keep coming back to: growth is the drumbeat businesses march to — new markets, new product lines, better operations — but it can strain resources and, poorly managed, endanger solvency. Free cash flow is the lifeblood that covers payroll, suppliers, and the next round of investment. The two are often at odds precisely when the growth case looks strongest.
>
> Growth signals ambition. Cash flow keeps the lights on. The CFO's craft is holding both without dropping either.
>
> It is as much an art as it is a science.
>
> (The full essay walks four of these trade-offs — with worked examples — on my site. Link on my profile.)

**Why this works:** opens with a real balance-sheet dilemma, not an announcement. The reader does the maths and takes a side, which is what drives comments. The "$3m the day you sign" line makes the abstraction concrete.

## LinkedIn — Post 2 (the four trade-offs framework)

> Growth and cash flow pull against each other in four predictable places. If you're a CFO, you already know all four:
>
> **Investment vs. liquidity.** Funding new initiatives drains the cash you need for short-term obligations.
>
> **Leverage vs. solvency.** Debt fuels growth. Too much of it endangers the firm. A renewable energy company I'd point to wanted a $15m facility to double capacity — with a debt-to-equity ratio already at 1:1, the high end for its sector.
>
> **Operational efficiency vs. expansion costs.** Scaling introduces inefficiency. A fast-growing fintech saw response times slow, fraud systems throw too many false positives, and manual review costs climb — all pressing on cash flow.
>
> **Strategic planning vs. market pressures.** Long-term bets don't yield near-term cash. A video game studio's new title needed heavy R&D and at least five years before a marketable product — a timeline that tests even visionary stakeholders.
>
> Notice the shape. Every row is the same fight: the thing that grows the firm is the thing that strains its cash.
>
> The CFO's role isn't to pick a side. It's to hold the balance long enough for the growth to pay for itself.
>
> (Full breakdown on my site — profile link.)

**Why this works:** a clean four-part framework is inherently restack-able, and each row carries a specific, real-industry example rather than a platitude. The "same fight" turn gives it a takeaway.

## LinkedIn — Post 3 (the short, punchy one)

> Two beacons guide corporate finance. They rarely point the same way.
>
> Growth says: enter the new market, build the new line, scale the operation.
>
> Cash flow says: make payroll, pay suppliers, stay solvent.
>
> For a CFO, the pursuit of growth is a sign of a firm's vitality. The management of cash flow is what keeps it alive to enjoy it.
>
> Get the balance wrong towards growth and you can endanger solvency. Get it wrong towards caution and you dilute the ambition that made the firm worth backing.
>
> This is a perennial challenge, not a problem you solve once. Worth asking which beacon your last three big decisions actually followed.
>
> (Longer version on my site — profile link.)

**Why this works:** two-part contrast, fast to read, ends on a self-reflective question that invites a comment without baiting for one.
