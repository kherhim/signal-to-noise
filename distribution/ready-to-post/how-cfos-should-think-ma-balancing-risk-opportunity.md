# Ready to post — How CFOs should think about M&A

*Canonical: https://signal-to-noise.co/insights/how-cfos-should-think-ma-balancing-risk-opportunity/*

M&A is a perennial high-interest topic for finance and strategy leaders, and this
piece is evergreen — a four-part framework rather than a hot take. That makes it
re-postable on any big deal announcement or deal-collapse story. These are finished

## LinkedIn — Post 1 (the flagship — the Buffett frame)

> Buffett said it best, in 1981, and it hasn't aged a day:
>
> "Investors can always buy toads at the going price for toads. If investors instead bankroll princesses who wish to pay double for the right to kiss the toad, those kisses had better pack some real dynamite. We've observed many kisses but very few miracles."
>
> That is the whole risk of M&A in three sentences.
>
> The question is almost never whether the target is a good business. It's whether the *premium* — what you pay over what the market already prices in — actually delivers value to your shareholders.
>
> In competitive markets it's easy to overpay. Valuations get inflated by hype and short-term trends. And once you've overpaid, no amount of integration heroics buys the difference back.
>
> The CFO is often the one person in the room with the standing to say so when the math says no. Not the loudest voice for the deal. The one prepared to be the brake.
>
> With rate cuts likely on both sides of the pond, cheaper financing will drive up animal spirits. More deals. More princesses. More kisses.
>
> Very few miracles.
>
> (I set out the four things every CFO should weigh — full essay on my site. Link on my profile.)

**Why this works:** the Buffett quote is a genuinely quotable, shareable object, and the "one person with the standing to say no" line flatters and challenges the target reader at once.

## LinkedIn — Post 2 (the "deals fail on the boring stuff" angle)

> M&A is sold as a fast track to growth, innovation and market expansion.
>
> Most deals that fail don't fail on the thesis. They fail on the parts nobody put on the pitch deck.
>
> Cultural misalignment is one of the leading causes of failed mergers. Great financials, a vastly different culture — and you get friction, lower morale, and the synergy benefits quietly evaporate.
>
> Then there's what due diligence misses when it stops at the income statement and balance sheet. Off-balance-sheet liabilities. Pending litigation. Tax exposures. Underfunded pensions. Working capital, which almost everyone overlooks until it's a liquidity problem. Key contracts that don't survive a change of ownership.
>
> And then integration — where the theoretical benefits are supposed to become real. Financial systems that won't talk to each other. Budgets and forecasts that were never reconciled. Key talent walking out the door because no one put a retention plan behind the handshake.
>
> The numbers get the attention. The deal gets won or lost on cultural fit, hidden liabilities, and the eighteen months *after* completion.
>
> A CFO's real job in M&A isn't to close the deal. It's to make sure the one you close was worth closing.
>
> (Full breakdown of all four areas on my site — profile link.)

**Why this works:** reframes M&A failure away from strategy (where it's usually blamed) toward the unglamorous execution risks a CFO actually owns. Specific and quietly alarming.

## LinkedIn — Post 3 (the short framework post)

> Four questions I'd want answered before signing off on any acquisition:
>
> **1. Does it fit the big picture?** Not "are the metrics attractive" — does it actually serve the long-term strategy, the culture, the market position? A traditional, slow-moving target can wreck an innovation company even with great numbers.
>
> **2. Have we priced the risk, or the hope?** Overvaluation, integration complexity, regulatory scrutiny. Stress-test the deal under different economic conditions before you fall in love with the synergy case.
>
> **3. What's hiding below the numbers?** Off-balance-sheet liabilities, shaky future cash flows, thin working capital, contracts that don't survive the deal.
>
> **4. Can we actually integrate it?** Systems, budgets, and — the one that quietly sinks deals — the people. Retention plan, or a slow leak of the talent you paid for.
>
> Strategic alignment. Risk management. Financial due diligence. Post-merger integration.
>
> Get one badly wrong and the other three can't save it.
>
> Worth asking which one your last deal actually tripped on.
>
> (Longer version on my site — profile link.)

**Why this works:** clean, saveable four-part checklist. Highly restack-able and it ends on a reflective prompt rather than a pitch.

## Substack Notes (space these over ~2 weeks)

**Note 1** — the Buffett pull-quote
> "We've observed many kisses but very few miracles." — Buffett, 1981, on companies paying a premium to acquire.
>
> The point isn't whether the target is a good business. It's whether the *premium* over what the market already prices in delivers anything to the acquirer's shareholders. Forty-odd years on, most M&A processes are still fighting this exact bias.

**Note 2** — the failure-mode observation
> Cultural misalignment is one of the leading causes of failed mergers.
>
> Not valuation. Not strategy. Culture. A company with great financials and a vastly different culture creates friction, drops morale, and quietly erases the synergies the whole deal was justified on. The number that kills the deal was never on the spreadsheet.

**Note 3** — question form, invites replies
> Genuine question for anyone who's run one: what actually tripped up your last M&A deal — the valuation, the diligence, the culture, or the integration?
>
> My wager is that far more deals die in the eighteen months *after* completion than in the negotiation. Post-merger integration is where the theory has to become real, and it's the part that gets the least planning.

**Note 4** — the diligence observation
> Financial due diligence that stops at the income statement and balance sheet isn't due diligence.
>
> The value-destroyers hide one layer down: off-balance-sheet liabilities, pending litigation, tax exposures, underfunded pensions, working capital nobody stress-tested, key contracts that don't survive a change of ownership. Numbers alone don't tell the full story — they tell the flattering half.

**Note 5** — restack + take
> (Restack Post 3 — the four-question checklist.)
> The framing I keep coming back to: strategic alignment, risk management, financial due diligence, post-merger integration. Get one badly wrong and the other three can't save the deal. M&A rarely fails for one reason — it fails because the CFO let one of the four slide.

## News-pegging (when to resurface this)

Resurface on any major M&A story. On a big deal *announcement*: "Everyone's
talking about the strategic fit. The number that decides this deal is the
*premium* — and whether those kisses pack any dynamite." On a deal *collapse* or
a write-down: "This is what overpaying, or botched integration, looks like on the
other side — the risk the CFO is supposed to price in up front." Also works on
rate-cut headlines ("cheaper financing, more animal spirits, more princesses") and
on any high-profile post-merger culture clash or talent exodus.
