## "The most expensive decision? 'Let's wait.'"

*Canonical: https://signal-to-noise.co/insights/2-most-expensive-decision-lets-wait/*

### LinkedIn — Post 1 (the flagship)

> The most expensive decision I've watched companies make is almost always the same one:
>
> "Let's wait."
>
> It never shows up in the numbers. There's no line item for the deal that drifted, the hire you lost, the market you entered a year late. Indecision is invisible — right up until it becomes a crisis.
>
> Companies lose more through hesitation than through the occasional wrong call. Because action compounds. And so does inaction.
>
> "Let's wait" feels safe in the room. It isn't. It quietly erodes momentum, talent, opportunity, and morale — none of which send you an invoice.
>
> As a CFO, I've learned to ask one question when someone reaches for it:
>
> **"What is the cost of *not* deciding?"**
>
> Most leaders never calculate it. But it's always there, and it's usually enormous.
>
> Bold decisions don't guarantee success. But indecision guarantees drift.
>
> (This is part of a series on the leadership lessons I wish I'd known earlier — full set on my site, link on my profile.)

**Why this works:** short, quotable, and it makes middle-managers who *love* "let's wait" want to defend themselves in the comments. That's engagement.

### Substack Notes
**Note A**
> The most expensive decision most companies make is "let's wait." It never hits the P&L — there's no line item for the deal that drifted or the hire you lost. Action compounds. So does inaction.

**Note B** — question form
> Honest question for the leaders here: when was the last time you actually calculated the cost of *not* deciding? Not the risk of the wrong call — the cost of the delay itself. Most of us never do. It's usually the bigger number.

## "When KPIs become the strategy, the strategy dies"

*Canonical: https://signal-to-noise.co/insights/5-when-kpis-become-strategy-dies/*

### LinkedIn — Post 1 (the flagship — the list is the hook)

> Four ways a metric quietly kills the thing it was meant to measure:
>
> → Revenue targets that kill profitability.
> → Cost targets that kill growth.
> → Efficiency targets that kill innovation.
> → Acquisition targets that destroy culture.
>
> I've watched all four happen. Every time, the same mechanism: the moment a team starts optimising the *metric* instead of the *mission*, you're on dangerous ground — and the numbers look great right up until they suddenly don't.
>
> Here's the part people get wrong about the CFO's job. It isn't to police the KPIs. It's to keep them *honest* — to keep the mission central and the metrics in service of it, not the other way round.
>
> A KPI is a good servant and a terrible master.
>
> (Part of my "leadership lessons" series — full set on my site, profile link.)

**Why this works:** the four-line list is scannable and instantly recognisable — everyone has watched at least one of these happen, and they'll tag a colleague.

### Substack Notes
**Note A** — the list
> Four ways a KPI kills what it measures:
> · Revenue targets that kill profitability
> · Cost targets that kill growth
> · Efficiency targets that kill innovation
> · Acquisition targets that destroy culture
>
> The moment the team optimises the metric instead of the mission, the numbers look great — until they suddenly don't.

**Note B**
> "The CFO's job isn't to police KPIs — it's to keep them honest." A KPI is a good servant and a terrible master. The mission stays central; the metrics serve it, never the reverse.
