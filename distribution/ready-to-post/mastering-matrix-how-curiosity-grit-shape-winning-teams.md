## LinkedIn — Post 1 (the flagship — the matrix)

> Stop hiring for the skill set. Start hiring for the mindset.
>
> In a world where change is the only constant, matching skills to roles isn't enough. The traits that let people thrive amid uncertainty — and still drive innovation forward — are curiosity and grit.
>
> Curiosity questions existing norms and imagines fresh opportunities. It's the force behind space exploration and a reshaped retail landscape. Grit is the relentless pursuit of long-term goals — the quality that turns ambitious concepts into finished outcomes.
>
> Plot them on a 2x2. Curiosity up one axis, grit across the other. You get four kinds of team member:
>
> High curiosity, high grit — Visionaries with determination. The innovators and the finishers.
> High curiosity, low grit — Ideators needing direction. Full of ideas; thrive when paired with grittier counterparts.
> Low curiosity, high grit — Executors with a mission. The ones you count on to get it done.
> Low curiosity, low grit — Seekers of motivation. Untapped potential, waiting on the right conditions.
>
> The point isn't to sort people and file them away. It's to see each person's real strengths and build the team around how the two traits combine.
>
> (Full matrix, and the strategies to build both traits, are on my site. Link on my profile.)

**Why this works:** opens with an instruction, not an announcement. Names the framework and hands the reader an immediately usable 2x2 with sticky quadrant labels. No link tax.

## LinkedIn — Post 2 (the "skills age fast" reframe — CFO lens)

> An uncomfortable line for anyone still hiring on a checklist of technical skills:
>
> The shelf life of a specific technical ability is diminishing rapidly.
>
> As artificial intelligence and technology keep moving, the skills you recruit for today age out faster than they used to. So the durable advantage isn't the skill itself. It's the capacity to acquire new skills when they're needed and let go of outdated ones.
>
> Two traits predict that capacity better than any qualification: curiosity and grit.
>
> Curiosity keeps people learning — anticipating change and innovating in response to emerging trends. Grit keeps them going when progress is slow and the obstacles look insurmountable.
>
> The essential future skill isn't mastering one particular expertise. It's learning to learn.
>
> Which changes the question you should be asking when you build a team: are you recruiting for what someone knows today, or for how fast they can grow into what you'll need next year?
>
> Curiosity and grit are the two you can least afford to skip.
>
> (The full argument — and how to cultivate both — is on my site. Profile link.)

**Why this works:** reframes a real, recognisable hiring cost, ties it straight to the framework, and ends on a self-audit question a leader can't easily dodge.

## LinkedIn — Post 3 (the short, punchy one)

> Two traits build teams that survive uncertainty. Most of the rest is replaceable.
>
> Curiosity — the willingness to question norms and chase ideas nobody has tried yet.
>
> Grit — the persistence to finish what curiosity starts.
>
> On their own, each has a ceiling. Curiosity without grit is a stream of ideas nobody sees through. Grit without curiosity is relentless execution of the same old thing.
>
> Together they're the dream team member: the innovator who is also the finisher.
>
> Most teams are long on one and short on the other. The fix isn't to replace people. It's to pair them — ideators with executors — and to deliberately grow the trait each person lacks.
>
> Worth asking which one your team is missing.
>
> (Longer version on my site — profile link.)

**Why this works:** clean two-part framework, highly restack-able, forces the reader to place their own team.

## Substack Notes (space these out)

**Note 1** — the matrix, standalone
> Picture a 2x2. Curiosity on one axis, grit on the other.
>
> High curiosity, high grit — Visionaries with determination: the innovators and the finishers.
> High curiosity, low grit — Ideators needing direction.
> Low curiosity, high grit — Executors with a mission.
> Low curiosity, low grit — Seekers of motivation: untapped potential.
>

**Note 2** — the skills line
> "In the ever-evolving landscape of artificial intelligence and technology, the longevity of specific technical abilities is diminishing rapidly."
>
> Which quietly demotes the skills checklist and promotes two things that don't age: the curiosity to keep learning, and the grit to keep going. Learning to learn is the future skill.

**Note 3** — question form (invites replies)
> Genuine question for anyone building a team right now: are you hiring for the skills someone has today, or for how fast they can acquire the next set — and drop the outdated ones?
>
> Because the first ages in months. The second — curiosity plus grit — doesn't.

**Note 4** — the "nobody's a write-off" observation
> The most useful thing about the curiosity–grit matrix: nobody is a write-off.
>
> Ideators lacking follow-through thrive when paired with grittier counterparts. Executors who don't seek out change can spark unexpected innovation once you invite them into new ideas. Even the low/low quadrant is untapped potential, not dead weight. The leader's job is placement, not judgement.
