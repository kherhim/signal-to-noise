# Ready to post — Once upon a balance sheet: 10 reasons storytelling is a CFO's secret superpower

*Canonical: https://signal-to-noise.co/insights/once-upon-balance-sheet-10-reasons-why-storytelling-cfos/*

A numbered "10 reasons" list on a soft skill for finance leaders is one of the
highest save-and-share formats going — fully readable without a click, easy to
argue with, easy to tag a colleague on. The premise (a CFO as narrator, not just
number-cruncher) travels well past finance, and the Ruth Porat example gives it a
real name to anchor to. Finished posts below, not scaffolds. Nothing in any post
body contains a link.

---

## LinkedIn — Post 1 (the flagship — the full list)

> In finance, numbers reign supreme. So here's an uncomfortable claim: the most underrated CFO skill isn't technical at all. It's storytelling.
>
> Ten reasons it's a superpower, not a soft skill.
>
> 1. Turning data into drama. Financial data is dry. A narrative makes complex concepts accessible to a broader audience.
> 2. Forecasts as future fables. Projections aren't just numbers — they're a vivid picture of where the company is headed.
> 3. The budget as a bestseller. Told well, a budget is less about spreadsheets, more about strategic priorities.
> 4. Audits as adventures. Compliance becomes a tale of diligence and integrity, not a necessary evil.
> 5. Earnings reports as epic tales. Each quarter a chapter building on the last, not a monotone.
> 6. Investor relations as narratives. Investors don't just buy numbers. They buy stories.
> 7. Crisis management as heroic sagas. Frame adversity as resilience, and you protect stakeholder confidence.
> 8. Change management as chronicles of transformation. Show the why, and transitions get smoother.
> 9. Culture crafting as folklore. Stories that carry the values shape the culture.
> 10. Persuasion as enchantment. Negotiating, funding, leading — it all runs on a compelling narrative.
>
> The modern CFO doesn't just balance books. They narrate the company's financial story.
>
> (Full essay on my site — link on my profile.)

**Why this works:** the whole list is readable with zero clicks, which is exactly what LinkedIn rewards. The "soft skill vs superpower" hook reframes a stereotype, and the numbered format invites people to argue which reason they'd cut.

## LinkedIn — Post 2 (the single sharpest reason — investors buy stories)

> A quiet truth about investor relations: investors aren't just interested in the numbers.
>
> They buy into the story.
>
> A CFO who can narrate the company's growth, its challenges and its future prospects compellingly is more likely to win and retain investor confidence than one who simply reads out the results.
>
> The clearest example I know is Ruth Porat at Alphabet. She's been acclaimed for articulating the company's financial story — breaking down revenue streams from advertising to cloud in a way a broad audience can actually follow.
>
> What makes it work isn't spin. It's structure. She connects the financials to the strategic vision: investments in AI, cloud, or Waymo get explained as long-term value creation, even when they dent short-term profit. She gives the context behind the numbers — not just the what, but the why and the how.
>
> That's the whole skill. Clarity, transparency, and a forward-looking read on where the business is headed.
>
> Numbers tell you what happened. A narrative tells investors what it means.
>
> (Full essay on my site — link on my profile.)

**Why this works:** takes one reason from the list and gives it room to breathe, then grounds it in a named, credible example. The Porat case makes the abstract claim concrete — and senior finance people restack things that name a peer they respect.

## LinkedIn — Post 3 (the contrarian frame — the CFO as narrator)

> Most people picture the CFO deep in a spreadsheet, crunching numbers and balancing the books.
>
> They're not wrong. They're just describing half the job.
>
> The other half is narration. The same financial data that reads as dry and dense on a slide can be turned into a narrative that makes a complex concept land with a broader audience.
>
> A crisis becomes a story about resilience and strategic prowess, not a panic. A period of change becomes a chronicle of transformation people can actually follow, which makes the transition smoother. A budget stops being a snooze-fest and starts being a case for strategic priorities.
>
> Same underlying facts. Completely different effect on the room.
>
> The persuasion is the point. Negotiating a deal, securing funding, guiding a team — a financial leader who can frame the numbers well moves people the raw figures never would.
>
> So next time you see a CFO deep in spreadsheets, consider they might be penning the next chapter of the company's story.
>
> (Full essay on my site — link on my profile.)

**Why this works:** the "half the job" reveal reframes a tired stereotype into something aspirational, which travels well past finance. It stacks three of the list's reasons into one argument without feeling like a list.

## News-pegging (when to resurface this)

Evergreen, so tie it to a moment. Best pegs: earnings season ("results week is
storytelling week — here's why the narrative matters as much as the numbers"), a
CFO who nails or fumbles an earnings call in the news, a big-tech results day
where a finance chief explains a heavy AI or cloud spend (peg straight to the
Porat example), or any "what makes a great investor update" think-piece doing the
rounds. Open with the peg, then drop the list: "Everyone's watching [X] explain
the numbers today — here are the ten reasons the story is the job."
