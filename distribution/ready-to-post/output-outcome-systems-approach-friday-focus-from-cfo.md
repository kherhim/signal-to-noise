# Ready to post — Output, outcome, and systems

*Canonical: https://signal-to-noise.co/insights/output-outcome-systems-approach-friday-focus-from-cfo/*

This one travels on a clean, debate-friendly distinction every operator already
half-holds: output ("what did we do?") versus outcome ("what did we achieve?"),
and then the quieter third move — systems, the processes that make success
inevitable. It's a framework people can argue with and apply the same afternoon.
These are finished, not scaffolds. Edit for your own ear, then post. Nothing
here contains a link in the body.

---

## LinkedIn — Post 1 (the flagship — the three lenses)

> Most teams measure what they did. Fewer measure what they achieved. Almost none build the thing that makes achieving it repeatable.
>
> There are three ways to measure success, and they're not rivals — they're a continuum.
>
> Output focus asks: what did we produce? Reports filed on time, revenue in the period, meetings held. Easy to count, because it's tangible. It works when the goal is straightforward and the path is clear.
>
> But lean on outputs alone in a complex environment and you get two failures I see constantly: misaligned incentives — a sales team chasing deal volume while ignoring profitability or risk — and short-term thinking, where hitting the quarter overshadows the recurring revenue you should be building.
>
> Outputs aren't the destination. They're mile markers along the way.
>
> Outcome focus asks a better question: what did we achieve? EBITDA margins, customer churn, market share. The Board never asks how many reports finance completed. They ask whether those reports drove decisions that moved the business.
>
> Both matter. But the one that compounds is the third — and I've put the full argument on my site.
>
> Which lens does your team actually run on?
>
> (Full essay on my site — link on my profile.)

**Why this works:** opens with an instruction-shaped claim, not an announcement. Names the two lenses everyone recognises, then withholds the third to earn the click. Ends on a question that invites people to place themselves.

## LinkedIn — Post 2 (the systems angle — the payoff lens)

> "What did we do?" is an output question. "What did we achieve?" is an outcome question.
>
> Here's the one most leaders skip: "What system would make this success inevitable?"
>
> A systems focus steps back from both milestones and results and concentrates on the processes and habits that drive them. Not isolated wins — continuous improvement.
>
> In corporate finance, systems are the backbone of resilience: a forecasting system that anticipates market shifts, cross-functional communication that keeps finance, operations and sales aligned, data systems that surface actionable insight in real time.
>
> Take M&A. An output-focused team tracks whether due diligence finished on time. An outcome-focused team asks whether the deal delivered its synergies and ROI. A systems-focused team asks something deeper: are our due diligence processes repeatable? Do we have a post-merger integration system that accelerates value? Is our communication transparent enough to align stakeholders?
>
> Invest in the system, and you're positioned to win not just this acquisition — but the next one.
>
> Outcomes fluctuate with the market. A strong system adapts and endures.
>
> (The full piece is on my site — profile link.)

**Why this works:** takes the same M&A example through all three lenses so the distinction lands concretely. The "make success inevitable" line is the quotable core, and the closing contrast reframes systems as the durable bet.

## LinkedIn — Post 3 (the short, punchy one)

> A checklist I keep for the three lenses:
>
> Output — what needs to get done?
> Outcome — what are we trying to achieve?
> System — what will make this repeatable and efficient?
>
> The sequencing matters. Start with outputs; they give immediate feedback — if finance is missing reporting deadlines, you have a tangible problem. Then align with outcomes: are those reports actually enabling better decisions? Then invest in systems, so the result holds year after year.
>
> Review outputs weekly. Outcomes quarterly. Systems annually.
>
> A sales team shouldn't be measured on revenue alone. Balance the output (revenue) with the outcome (customer satisfaction) and the system (adherence to the CRM, and sensible cost to generate that revenue).
>
> Most scorecards stop at the first line.
>
> (Longer version on my site — profile link.)

**Why this works:** a clean three-line framework plus a review cadence people can copy straight into their own operating rhythm. Restack-able and immediately usable.

## Substack Notes (space these over ~2 weeks)

**Note 1** — the core distinction
> Output focus asks "what did we do?" Outcome focus asks "what did we achieve?"
>
> They sound similar. They aren't. An output-focused M&A team celebrates finishing due diligence on time. An outcome-focused one asks whether the acquisition actually delivered its synergies and ROI. Same deal, entirely different definition of success.

**Note 2** — the mile-markers line
> "Outputs are important, but they are not the destination — they're mile markers along the way."
>
> The trap is that outputs are the easiest thing to measure, so they quietly become the thing you optimise. Number of reports. Deal volume. Meetings held. All countable, all beside the point if they aren't moving an outcome.

**Note 3** — systems, plainly (question form, invites replies)
> Genuine question for anyone building a team: are you optimising what you achieve, or building the system that makes achieving it inevitable?
>
> A systems focus stops asking "what do we want to achieve?" and starts asking "what must we build to make success inevitable?" What's one system in your function that quietly does that — or should?

**Note 4** — standalone observation
> The Board doesn't ask how many reports the finance team completed. They ask whether those reports drove insights that changed the business.
>
> That single reframe — from activity to impact — is the whole distance between an output culture and an outcome one.

**Note 5** — restack + take
> (Restack Post 2 — the systems one.)
> The line I keep returning to: outcomes fluctuate with market conditions, but a strong system adapts and endures. A CFO driven by outputs chases reporting accuracy. One driven by outcomes chases strategic insight. A systems-focused one asks what tools, processes and people deliver both — regardless of external pressure.

Resurface whenever the cycle serves up a metrics-and-targets story: an earnings
