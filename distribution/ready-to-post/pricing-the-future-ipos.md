## LinkedIn — Post 1 (the scoreboard post — the framework called it)

> Two months ago SpaceX pulled off the largest IPO in history. $75 billion raised. A $2 trillion valuation. A 19% first-day pop.
>
> By late July the stock was trading below its offer price. Today it sits beneath the price it opened at on day one — after a quarter where revenue beat expectations.
>
> The business didn't change. The hype premium washed out. And that was always the least interesting part of the story.
>
> In May, before the listing, I argued these IPOs should be read through three lenses instead of the valuation headlines:
>
> **Capital intensity.** The question was never "how big is the raise" — it's the free-cash-flow trajectory that justifies the multiple. Musk himself has warned holders to expect volatility while the company spends heavily on long-dated projects. The market has spent two months repricing exactly that.
>
> **Governance complexity.** Founder control and dual-class structures shape index eligibility and, eventually, cost of capital. That discount doesn't show up on day one. It shows up in month two.
>
> **Partner dependency.** The lens that barely applied to SpaceX — it's vertically integrated — but is the whole question for the two listings behind it.
>
> Because the queue is still forming: Anthropic filed confidentially in June, with investors reportedly targeting a $2 trillion debut as soon as October — larger than SpaceX. OpenAI has filed too, and is reportedly weighing a slip to 2027.
>
> Buying the hype is easy. Reading the cap table is the job.
>
> (The May breakdown of all three, side by side, is on my site — link on my profile.)

**Why this works:** the framework was published *before* the event and the event
obeyed it — that's the strongest credibility position available. The scoreboard
opener (record IPO → below offer in six weeks) is a concrete, arguable hook in
the 40p-NDA mould.

## LinkedIn — Post 2 (the single-question post — aimed at the next two listings)

> One question separates a real AI platform from an expensive R&D lab:
>
> **Where does the economic value actually accrue?**
>
> At the foundation-model layer? Inside the cloud partnership? In downstream enterprise contracts?
>
> Two AI companies have now filed confidentially for listings. One is reportedly targeting a $2 trillion debut — which would make it the largest IPO in history, past SpaceX's record from June — while leaning on hyperscalers for both capital and infrastructure. The other is so deeply integrated with a single strategic partner that its corporate-structure conversion is part of why the listing may slip to 2027.
>
> For both, value accrual isn't a philosophical question. It's the whole prospectus. If the value accrues to the partner, you're underwriting someone else's moat.
>
> And SpaceX just supplied the control experiment: no meaningful partner dependency, vertical integration, a revenue beat — and the market *still* spent two months squeezing the hype premium out. Now imagine the same repricing applied to a business whose infrastructure belongs to someone else.
>
> It's the test I'd apply to every AI company heading for public markets — and to a lot of the private ones too.
>
> (Full three-company breakdown on my site — profile link.)

**Why this works:** one sharp question, now with a live control experiment
attached. The "underwriting someone else's moat" line is the quotable.
