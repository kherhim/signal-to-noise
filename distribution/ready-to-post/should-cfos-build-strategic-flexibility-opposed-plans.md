# Ready to post — Should CFOs build strategic flexibility as opposed to strategic plans?

*Canonical: https://signal-to-noise.co/insights/should-cfos-build-strategic-flexibility-opposed-plans/*

This one travels because it sits on a debate leaders already argue about: strategy
versus rigid planning. The clean split — planning is actions given an expected
outcome, preparation is actions given deviations from it — gives people a frame
they can quote and disagree with. These are finished, not scaffolds. Edit for
your own ear, then post. Nothing here contains a link in the body.

---

## LinkedIn — Post 1 (the flagship — the definition split)

> A strategic plan is a set of actions given an expected outcome.
>
> Strategic preparation is a set of actions given deviations from that outcome.
>
> Most finance functions are heavy on the first and light on the second. That's the wrong balance for the environment we're actually operating in.
>
> Planning gives you direction. It's goal-focused, step-by-step, time-specific — and it leans on forecasts about market trends and internal capability. That reliance on forecasts is exactly what makes it fragile when the external environment moves fast.
>
> Preparation gives you adaptability. It plans for multiple potential outcomes rather than a single predicted future. It builds skills, identifies vulnerabilities, sets contingencies, and recalibrates in real time as conditions change.
>
> During COVID, the businesses with adaptable operations fared significantly better than those locked to rigid plans. That wasn't luck. It was readiness.
>
> Here's the part worth holding onto: planning and preparation aren't opposing forces. They complement each other. The CFO's job is to integrate both — a roadmap you can follow, and the capacity to act well when the roadmap stops matching the terrain.
>
> Plans change. Readiness stays.
>
> (I wrote the long version on my site. Link's on my profile.)

**Why this works:** opens with a crisp definition, not an announcement. The planning-vs-preparation split is quotable and gives readers something to argue with. Ends on a memorable line, not a link.

## LinkedIn — Post 2 (the CFO-uncertainty angle)

> As a CFO, you're often tasked with steering the organisation through uncertainty. Planning alone won't get you there.
>
> Markets are volatile — economic shifts, geopolitical developments, technological advances. A plan built on a single predicted future is one surprise away from irrelevance.
>
> Preparation is what lets you pivot without panic. Concretely, that looks like:
>
> Stress-testing your financial models before the shock, not during it.
>
> Securing flexible credit lines so cash isn't the constraint when conditions turn.
>
> Building redundancies in the supply chain so one broken link doesn't stop the line.
>
> Running optimistic, pessimistic and baseline scenarios instead of one forecast you quietly hope holds.
>
> None of this replaces planning. It sits alongside it. Planning gives clarity; preparation gives the ability to adapt and thrive when clarity runs out.
>
> Preparation also does something planning can't. It identifies risks before they become problems — and it inspires confidence. Investors and employees value organisations that show foresight and readiness. That trust is an asset in itself.
>
> Ask yourself honestly: have you built a plan, or have you built the capacity to act when the plan breaks?
>
> (Full essay on my site — profile link.)

**Why this works:** turns an abstract idea into four concrete CFO moves a finance leader recognises. The closing question forces self-reflection and invites comment.

## LinkedIn — Post 3 (the short, punchy one)

> Two questions sit behind every strategy.
>
> **Planning** asks: what do we want to achieve, and how?
>
> **Preparation** asks: what happens when it doesn't go the way we predicted?
>
> Planning is structured and linear. It operates within set timelines and relies on expected scenarios. Preparation is dynamic and iterative. It treats time as fluid and assumes uncertainty from the start.
>
> Both matter. But in a fast-changing environment, the second question is the one that keeps you competitive when the first one's answer stops working.
>
> Plans may change. Readiness remains a constant asset.
>
> Worth asking which question your last strategy off-site actually spent its time on.
>
> (Longer version on my site — profile link.)

**Why this works:** clean two-part framework, highly restack-able, ends by making the reader audit their own process.

## News-pegging (when to resurface this)

Resurface on any peg that exposes a rigid plan meeting a fast-moving world: a
supply-chain shock, a sudden rate move, a raw-material spike, a geopolitical
jolt, or a company visibly caught flat-footed by an event it didn't forecast.
Open with the news, pivot to the frame: "Everyone's reacting to [X]. The firms
that prepared for deviations, not just the expected outcome, are the ones
pivoting today."
