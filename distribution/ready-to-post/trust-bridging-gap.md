# Ready to post — Trust: bridging the gap

*Canonical: https://signal-to-noise.co/insights/trust-bridging-gap/*

This one travels furthest of any piece in the corpus, because it isn't really
about finance — it's about trust in teams and leadership, a theme every reader
carries into work regardless of function. The Trust Equation gives it a named,
quotable frame, and the "gap between words and actions" is a line anyone who has
worked for a manager will recognise. These are finished, not scaffolds. Edit for
your own ear, then post. Nothing here contains a link in the body.

---

## LinkedIn — Post 1 (the flagship — lead with the equation)

> Trust has a formula, and Harvard Business School wrote it down.
>
> Trust = (Credibility + Reliability + Intimacy) / Self-Orientation.
>
> Read it slowly. Three things sit on top. Credibility — how believable you are when you speak. Reliability — whether you can be counted on to act. Intimacy — whether people can confide in you without fear.
>
> And one thing sits underneath, dividing all of it: self-orientation. How focused you are on yourself rather than on the people in front of you.
>
> That placement is the whole point. Self-orientation is a denominator. The more of it you carry, the more it shrinks everything above it. You can be credible, dependable and safe to confide in — and still corrode trust the moment people sense you're in it for yourself.
>
> A low self-orientation raises trust. A high one lowers it, no matter how good the rest of your numbers look.
>
> Most trust problems I've watched up close weren't credibility problems. They were denominator problems.
>
> (I wrote the long version — including where the gap between words and actions does the most damage — on my site. Link's on my profile.)

**Why this works:** opens with a claim, not an announcement, then hands the reader a framework they can quote. The "denominator problem" reframe is memorable and forces self-reflection. No link tax.

## LinkedIn — Post 2 (the words-vs-actions angle)

> Trust isn't broken by what leaders say. It's broken by the distance between what they say and what they do.
>
> A manager tells the team: hit these targets and the bonus is yours. The targets get hit. Then a reason arrives not to pay. Then another.
>
> The words were fine. It's the gap that did the damage.
>
> And it doesn't stay contained. Once the team stops trusting the promise, they stop trusting the manager — and then they start re-reading everything else management has ever said. Disengagement. Lost motivation. A quiet audit of every other claim.
>
> The same fracture runs through stated values. A company can print integrity and respect on the wall, then pressure people into unpaid overtime, and every employee reads the contradiction instantly. The mission statement doesn't build credibility. The gap between it and the behaviour destroys it.
>
> Trust is made or broken in that gap. Not in the promise — in the delivery.
>
> Which is why consistency isn't a soft virtue. Every action either adds to your trustworthiness or subtracts from it. There is no neutral.
>
> (Full essay on my site — link on my profile.)

**Why this works:** the bonus example is concrete and near-universal — most readers have lived it. The "quiet audit of everything else" line names a dynamic people feel but rarely articulate.

## LinkedIn — Post 3 (the short, punchy one)

> If a promise can't be kept, say so.
>
> Most trust damage doesn't come from breaking a commitment. It comes from breaking it in silence — letting the gap sit there, unexplained, until people fill it with their own worst guess.
>
> Reliability is built slowly, one kept commitment at a time. So make commitments carefully. And when circumstances change and one can't hold, communicate it openly: the reason, and an alternative.
>
> That's it. Openness about a broken promise costs less trust than the promise did.
>
> The leaders people trust aren't the ones who never miss. They're the ones who never leave you guessing.
>
> Worth asking which one you've been.
>
> (Longer version on my site — profile link.)

**Why this works:** opens with an instruction, stays tight, and lands on a self-directed question. Extremely restack-able because it's a single clean principle.

## Substack Notes (space these over ~2 weeks)

**Note 1** — the equation, standalone
> Trust = (Credibility + Reliability + Intimacy) / Self-Orientation.
>
> Three things build it — how believable you are, how dependable you are, how safe you are to confide in. One thing divides all three: how focused you are on yourself. Self-orientation is the denominator, and denominators are ruthless. A high one shrinks everything above it.

**Note 2** — the gap
> "The gap between what is said and what is done is where trust is most vulnerable."
>
> Not the words. Not even the intent. The distance between the two. Leaders make promises and miss them; they name values and contradict them. Every reader clocks the discrepancy long before anyone says it out loud.

**Note 3** — the closing line
> "Trust is not something that can be demanded or assumed; it must be earned through consistent, authentic actions that align with one's words."
>
> A whole leadership philosophy fits in that sentence. You can't announce trust into existence. You can only accumulate it, one aligned action at a time.

**Note 4** — the self-orientation point (question form, invites replies)
> Genuine question. Think of a leader you stopped trusting. Was it really that they were unreliable or unbelievable — or was it that you sensed they were in it for themselves?
>
> In the Trust Equation, self-orientation sits in the denominator for a reason. High self-focus corrodes trust even when credibility and reliability are intact. How much trust do you think is actually lost there, versus everywhere else?

**Note 5** — restack + take
> (Restack Post 2 — the words-vs-actions one.)
> The line I keep coming back to: when someone stops trusting one promise, they don't stop there. They quietly re-audit everything else you've ever told them. That's why a single unexplained broken commitment costs so much more than it looks like it should.
