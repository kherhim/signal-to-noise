## LinkedIn — Post 1 (the scannable cheat sheet — built to be saved)

> Where an LLM actually earns its place in a finance function. A working list:
>
> **Forecasting** — pulls in real-time data, industry trends and economic indicators, and flags the patterns and anomalies a spreadsheet won't.
>
> **Cash flow** — predicts trends from history plus current conditions; automates invoice processing and payment scheduling.
>
> **Routine processes** — transaction handling to compliance reporting, done consistently. It scales as you grow without scaling headcount at the same rate.
>
> **Analysis and reporting** — surfaces hidden patterns; produces customised reports tailored to each stakeholder; runs in real time.
>
> **Scenario planning** — evaluates multiple "what ifs" at once, so contingency plans stop being an annual exercise.
>
> **Investor relations** — real-time performance updates, and — done well — interactive Q&A on the numbers.
>
> None of this replaces the CFO. It's what frees one to stop being a scorekeeper and start being a Chief Value Officer.
>
> Save this one. You'll want the list in front of you the next time someone asks "what would we even use it for?"

## LinkedIn — Post 2 (the risk / governance angle)

> The exciting part of LLMs in finance gets all the airtime. The part that decides whether it works gets almost none.
>
> Three things I'd get right before the first forecast:
>
> **Your data must not become part of the model.** You do not want your company's idiosyncratic financial data quietly absorbed into a tool you don't control. Encrypt it. Understand exactly where it goes.
>
> **Fewer hands, not more.** Think hard and limit access. A smaller team touching both the financial data and the LLM means a lower risk of garbage in, garbage out. Access control is a quality control.
>
> **Compliance is a moving target.** New technology plus evolving standards means you're auditing against a benchmark that keeps shifting. That's ongoing work, not a launch task.
>
> And the honest one underneath all three: LLMs evolve much faster than GAAP. The tool changes under you every few months. Your governance has to assume that, not resist it.
>
> The strategic edge is real. It only survives contact with a finance function that took the boring parts seriously first.
>

## LinkedIn — Post 3 (the change-management reality check)

> The technology is the easy bit. The people are the implementation.
>
> Most LLM rollouts in finance don't stall on the model. They stall on three things nobody costed:
>
> Training a team whose default stance is *running a process*, not experimenting with one. That's a genuine shift in posture, and it's harder than any tutorial makes it sound.
>
> Change management that asks people to unlearn old norms and learn new ones — the slowest kind of change there is.
>
> Support that's good enough. The technology is new and your users are newer. Low-quality support can quietly kill the whole implementation.
>
> There's a commercial thread through all of it: when you pick a vendor, wear a commercial hat and understand the economics. "Experience" in something this new is scarce — but some vendors have more of it than others.
>
> The firms that win with LLMs won't be the ones with the best model. They'll be the ones that treated adoption as a people problem and resourced it like one.
>

## Substack Notes

**Note 1** — the reframe
> A CFO's real question about LLMs isn't "can it forecast." It's "how good can I be if I can't move from scorekeeper to Chief Value Officer?"
>
> The tools aren't the point. What they free you up to become is.

**Note 2** — the governance line, standalone
> "You don't want your company's idiosyncratic data to become 'a part' of the LLM you're using."
>
> The most important sentence in any finance AI rollout, and the one that gets skipped in every excited demo.

**Note 3** — the GIGO point
> A smaller team with access to both the financial data and the LLM means a lower risk of garbage in, garbage out.
>
> Counter-intuitive but right: with these tools, restricting access *is* a quality control, not just a security one.

**Note 4** — question form (invites replies)
> Genuine question for finance leaders trialling LLMs: what's actually stalling you — the technology, or the change management?
>
> Every rollout I look at trips on the second one. People have to unlearn old norms and learn new ones, and that's the slowest change there is. Curious whether that matches your experience.

**Note 5** — the line I keep coming back to
> "LLMs evolve much faster than GAAP."
>
> Which means the hard part isn't adopting the tool once. It's building a team with a continuous-learning mindset, because the thing you adopted will have changed by next quarter.
