This one has the single best hook in the corpus for a finance audience: the

## LinkedIn — Post 1 (the flagship — lead with the number)

> A frontier model reviews a standard NDA for about 40 pence.
>
> A paralegal on £45k does the same review for roughly £24 of loaded time — plus the two-day queue in legal's inbox that's quietly holding up the deal.
>
> Sixty-fold cheaper. And almost entirely beside the point.
>
> Because 40p is the cost of the *tokens*, not the cost of the *outcome*. The model's output is probabilistic, so someone senior still spot-checks it — ten minutes of a solicitor's time, £15+. Add the escalation path for the reviews it gets wrong, and the cost of designing the checking process at all, and a genuinely trustworthy review lands around £8–15.
>
> Still far cheaper. Still dramatically faster. But the number that matters — cost-to-outcome — was never on the token invoice.
>
> This is the correction most finance functions still need to make. The token line is procurement. "What does it cost to reach a trustworthy outcome when part of the thinking is rented?" is strategy.
>
> The token bill is noise. Cost-to-outcome is the signal.
>
> (Full breakdown — three pools of capacity, and why this capacity isn't actually yours — on my site. Link on my profile.)

**Why this works:** the 40p vs £24 contrast is irresistible to the target reader, and the "beside the point" turn keeps them reading past the hook into the actual argument.

## LinkedIn — Post 2 (the balance-sheet ghost)

> Here's something that should bother more CFOs than it does:
>
> A company can now materially expand its thinking capacity and leave *no trace* on the balance sheet.
>
> No asset. No depreciation schedule. No headcount line. Just a subscription and a usage charge dissolving quietly into operating costs.
>
> The capacity is real. The accounting is nearly silent.
>
> We've half-seen this before — cloud computing did it to servers. But cloud replaced *machines*. This reshapes *work itself*. It sits as a third pool of capacity, between people (embodied judgement, lumpy, path-dependent) and software (frozen logic, capex-shaped). Pure variable opex, expensed as consumed, infinitely elastic.
>
> And the elasticity is the trap. People came with friction — hiring, training, management span — and that friction was also a brake. Nobody accidentally hired forty analysts. Metered cognition has no such brake. So you get a machine-written post-mortem on every ticket, a summary of every meeting, a report nobody reads. The bill grows; the stock of decisions actually improved by it doesn't.
>
> The constraint has moved from *availability* to *design*. Not "can we afford this capacity" but "where in the system does it belong, and which outcomes justify any spend at all."
>
> That's a harder question. And it can't be answered by a rate card.
>
> (The full piece is on my site — profile link.)

**Why this works:** "leaves no trace on the balance sheet" is a genuinely unsettling claim to a finance leader. Jevons paradox without the jargon.

## LinkedIn — Post 3 (the sole-supplier reframe — most contrarian)

> Most companies are treating their AI usage like a software subscription.
>
> It's a sole-supplier dependency for a critical input, and it should be governed like one.
>
> Think about what you actually control. When capacity is people, you have levers — retention, contracts, culture. When it's a perpetual software licence, version 11 keeps behaving like version 11 for as long as you run it.
>
> Metered cognition offers neither. The vendor can reprice it. They can deprecate the exact model your workflow was tuned to — on cycles of months, not years. They can change how it behaves with a mid-quarter update, without asking you. The characteristics of your capacity can shift without your consent, and there's no version you can pin and own.
>
> Now layer the probabilistic output on top. The right mental model isn't "SaaS vendor." It's single-source supplier of a critical component.
>
> Which means the discipline is supply-chain discipline: second-sourcing, an abstraction layer so a model swap is a config change and not a rebuild, fallback paths for the processes that can't tolerate an outage.
>
> Very few firms have made that connection yet. The ones that do will look a lot less exposed in eighteen months.
>
> (Full essay on my site — link on my profile.)

**Why this works:** takes a thing everyone does casually and reframes it as a governance risk they're ignoring. Provocative, specific, actionable.
