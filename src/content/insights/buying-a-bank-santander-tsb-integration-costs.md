Santander [agreed to buy TSB from Sabadell](https://www.santander.co.uk/about-santander/media-centre/press-releases/santander-to-acquire-tsb-from-sabadell-for-ps265) in July 2025 for £2.65bn in cash. The same announcement carried a second number that got less attention: £520m of pre-tax restructuring costs during 2026 and 2027.

That second number is the one a CFO should study. The headline price buys the bank. The integration budget decides whether the purchase pays.

The deal [completed on 30 April 2026](https://www.santander.co.uk/about-santander/media-centre/press-releases/santander-uk-completes-cash-acquisition-of-tsb-banking). TSB customers move onto Santander's systems in the second half of 2027. So the expensive part has not started yet, and the checklist below is one you can run on any acquisition before that point.

## Add the integration bill to the price

Santander paid £2.65bn [plus about £213m](https://www.santander.co.uk/about-santander/media-centre/press-releases/santander-uk-completes-cash-acquisition-of-tsb-banking) for the change in TSB's tangible net asset value between April 2025 and completion. Add the £520m of restructuring and the all-in cost is about £3.38bn.

The restructuring alone is 20% of the headline price. On a board paper it sits in a different section from the consideration, often on a different page. It belongs in the same sum.

## Divide the one-off cost by the run-rate saving

Santander [expects cost synergies](https://www.santander.co.uk/about-santander/media-centre/press-releases/santander-to-acquire-tsb-from-sabadell-for-ps265) of at least £400m a year, which it puts at 13% of the combined cost base, with most of them realised by 2027. Spending £520m to save £400m a year is a ratio of 1.3.

The ratio tells you two things. Below one, the integration pays for itself inside a year of full savings. Well above two, the savings case usually rests on assumptions nobody has tested. At 1.3, Santander sits in the range where the plan is credible, provided the timing holds.

## Lay the promises over the timetable

Here the deal gets interesting. Three dates sit on [TSB's own customer page](https://www.santander.co.uk/personal/TSB-acquisition):

- **The legal transfer:** A High Court hearing on 15 February 2027, with the transfer effective on 22 March 2027 if approved.
- **The systems migration:** Accounts and services move to Santander platforms in the second half of 2027.
- **The branch promise:** No further Santander or TSB branch closures before 2028 at the earliest.

Most of the £400m is due by 2027. The migration lands late in 2027. Branches, one of the obvious pools of savings in a retail bank merger, are off the table until 2028.

So the 2027 savings have to come from head office, duplicated functions and suppliers, and they have to arrive while the bank runs two platforms. A CFO reading this plan would ask for the synergy bridge by quarter and by source, and would expect the year-two number to slip.

## Price the tail, not just the budget

TSB has done this before. In April 2018 it moved 5.2 million customers onto a new IT platform. The [FCA's final notice](https://www.fca.org.uk/news/press-releases/tsb-fined-ps4865m-operational-resilience-failings) records that all of TSB's branches and a significant share of its customers were affected, and that the bank took until December 2018 to return to business as usual.

The measurable costs were £32.7m of redress and £48.65m of fines, which would have been £69.5m without the settlement discount. Those are the lines that reach the accounts. Eight months of management attention, lost customers and a damaged brand do not have a line.

The regulators found that TSB "failed to organise and control the IT migration programme adequately" and failed to manage the risks of its outsourcing to a critical supplier. Neither failure shows up in a restructuring budget. Both decide whether the budget is enough.

For any integration with a systems migration, a CFO should hold a contingency outside the restructuring figure and size it against a bad migration, not an average one.

## Check the returns after the one-off costs

Santander [expects the deal](https://www.santander.co.uk/about-santander/media-centre/press-releases/santander-to-acquire-tsb-from-sabadell-for-ps265) to earn a return on invested capital above 20% and to lift Santander UK's return on tangible equity from 11% in 2024 to 16% by 2028. It consumes about 50 basis points of group CET1 capital.

Those returns are run-rate figures. Ask for the same calculation with the £520m in the denominator and with synergies a year late. If the deal still clears the cost of capital, the board has a margin of safety. If it only clears it on the base case, the board has bought an option on perfect execution.

## Ask who has done it before

Santander's announcement points to its own record. It integrated Abbey after 2004, and Alliance & Leicester and Bradford & Bingley after 2008. That experience is a genuine asset. It also describes a different bank, different systems and different people from the ones that will run the 2027 migration.

The useful question asks who in the current team ran the last migration, and whether they are still there for this one.

## What the deal teaches

Paying [1.45 times tangible book](https://www.santander.co.uk/about-santander/media-centre/press-releases/santander-to-acquire-tsb-from-sabadell-for-ps265) for a bank with about five million customers and £35bn of deposits can be a good price. Whether it was a good price depends on £520m spent well, in the right order, by people who have done it before.

Every acquisition has two prices. Boards negotiate the first one hard. The second one needs the same scrutiny before signing, because after completion it can only go up.
