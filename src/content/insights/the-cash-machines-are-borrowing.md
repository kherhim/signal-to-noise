In the first half of 2025 Alphabet spent $28.3 billion buying back its own shares. In the first half of 2026 it spent nothing. It went to the markets for money instead. The second-quarter cash flow statement records $24.8 billion of new debt, $19.1 billion of mandatory convertible preferred stock and a common share issue. Long-term debt more than doubled over six months, rising from $46.5 billion to $98.2 billion ([Alphabet Q2 2026 release](https://s206.q4cdn.com/479360582/files/doc_financials/2026/q2/2026q2-alphabet-earnings-release.pdf)). In the quarter it spent $44.9 billion on capital projects and took in $39.1 billion from operations. Free cash flow turned negative for the first time since the company listed in 2004.

Alphabet has company. Meta reported last Wednesday. It generated $32.8 billion of third-quarter operating cash flow and spent $34.9 billion on capital projects, producing negative free cash flow of $2.1 billion, compared with positive $10.6 billion a year earlier ([Meta Q3 2026 release](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/meta-reports-third-quarter-2026-results-302915964.html)). Meta has repurchased no shares in 2026. Since December, its long-term debt has climbed from $58.7 billion to $80.9 billion and now nearly matches its $82.5 billion of cash and securities. Amazon recorded free cash flow outflow of $7.6 billion over the twelve months to June, compared with inflow of $18.2 billion a year earlier ([Amazon Q2 2026 release](https://ir.aboutamazon.com/news-release/news-release-details/2026/Amazon-com-Announces-Second-Quarter-Results/)).

For two decades these were the best cash machines ever built. They earned more than they could spend, handed the surplus back through buybacks, and had to explain to investors why they held so much cash. That period ended this year, quietly, in the cash flow statements.

## A market afraid of both directions

The Nasdaq dropped 2.6% on Thursday, its steepest decline since June, while the Philadelphia semiconductor index dropped 6.7%. Meta fell almost 7% ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2026/10/23/us-tech-stocks-tumble-as-investors-fret-over-ai-spending)). The explanations point in opposite directions.

One camp says the spending is too big. Meta raised the bottom of its 2026 capital budget to a range of $135 to $145 billion and went cash negative to pay for it. The other camp says the spending is starting to slow. Tesla lowered its 2026 capital spending guidance from more than $25 billion to more than $20 billion. Gordon Johnson of GLJ Research wrote that "Many AI bulls have long said the first 'hyperscaler' CAPEX cut = the end of the AI trade" ([Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/technology/ai/articles/tesla-just-reversed-2026-capex-003005120.html)). Jay Goldberg of Seaport Research offered the clearest account: "In short, nothing has changed. Investors have just decided to be afraid this week" ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2026/10/23/us-tech-stocks-tumble-as-investors-fret-over-ai-spending)).

Both camps miss the shift underneath. The market still values these companies as software businesses, and in cash terms they now behave like utilities.

## Software economics, utility economics

A software business pays once to build a product and sells it many times. Growth needs little capital, profits turn into cash, and cash pays for buybacks. Investors pay up for growth because growth costs so little.

A utility works the other way. It spends heavily before any revenue arrives, borrows to do it, and lives on the spread between what its assets earn and what its capital costs. Nobody values a utility on growth alone, because growth eats cash. The question is what each new dollar of assets earns, and for how long.

The hyperscalers moved from the first model to the second in about eighteen months. In the four quarters to March, the big four bought $434 billion of property and equipment and recognised about $149 billion of depreciation ([Silicon Analysts](https://siliconanalysts.com/analysis/hyperscaler-ai-capex-depreciation-wall-2026)). Most of that gap reaches the income statement over the next five or six years. Microsoft, Alphabet and Amazon each extended server useful lives to six years between 2022 and 2024, reducing depreciation and increasing reported profit. Amazon later reduced the life of part of its fleet, citing "an increased pace of technology development, particularly in the area of artificial intelligence" ([Silicon Analysts](https://siliconanalysts.com/analysis/hyperscaler-ai-capex-depreciation-wall-2026)). The accounts assume a GPU earns for six years. The pace at which chips are replaced suggests a shorter life.

Buffett made the point decades ago. The best business needs little capital to grow, and the worst needs a lot and earns little on it. This summer, I wrote about his test for [capital allocation](/insights/buffett-on-capital-allocation/) and for [buybacks](/insights/ask-warren-buyback-multiple/). The test still applies here, and the answer is awkward. For now nobody outside these companies knows what the new assets will earn.

## What it means for the buyer

Most CFOs reading this do not own a data centre. They buy from the companies that do, and for them this matters more than any share price.

A supplier paying its way from surplus cash can cut prices to win share and wait for the returns. A supplier funded by bonds and preferred stock needs its assets busy, because idle capacity still carries interest and depreciation. Expect the contracts to change. Committed annual spend, minimum volumes and multi-year terms keep the supplier's capacity full, and the buyer carries the risk. Google Cloud reported a backlog of $514 billion in July and AWS $496 billion. Those are the suppliers' assets, and their customers' commitments.

Before signing a multi-year AI contract, a CFO should ask what happens if the price falls by half during the term, as token prices have done this year, and who keeps the difference. I wrote about this risk in [budgeting for deflation](/insights/budgeting-for-deflation/). The new funding model makes suppliers keener to lock buyers in.

## What it means for the investor

For venture investors the change runs through the whole stack. Every AI application company rents compute from these four. The platforms used to pay for the build-out from free cash, and the startups above them enjoyed falling prices paid for by someone else's surplus. That surplus has gone. The bond market now pays for the build-out, at a cost of capital that moves with rates and mood, as Thursday showed.

None of this makes the boom a bubble. Meta's revenue rose 26% last quarter and Google Cloud grew 82% in the quarter before. It does mean the cost of capital now reaches the top of the stack. A valuation that assumes cheap, plentiful compute for ever rests on something the suppliers themselves no longer assume.

## Wednesday night

Microsoft and Alphabet are due to report after the close in New York this evening, and Amazon tomorrow. Four lines will matter more than the headline profit:

1. Capital spending against operating cash flow, quarter and trailing year.
2. Share repurchases, and whether any resume.
3. New debt and equity issued, in the financing section of the cash flow statement.
4. Depreciation growth against revenue growth.

If the first number stays above one and the second stays at zero, the change is permanent, and the market will have to price these companies for what they have become. The best cash machines in history now borrow to grow. That may still pay off handsomely. The money just comes from somewhere else.
