In 1965, the British mathematician I. J. Good, who had worked alongside Alan Turing deciphering German wartime codes at Bletchley Park, put a sentence on paper that still ticks away. He argued that the first ultraintelligent machine would be humanity's last necessary invention, provided it remained sufficiently docile to explain how we could retain control.

**Provided.** Sixty years later, that single word carries a heavier burden than anything else in the language.

Companies in San Francisco, London and Beijing are now building what Good envisaged, while freely acknowledging that they cannot explain what they are creating. People around dinner tables ask whether we could control a superintelligence. They are folding two questions into one.

The first asks whether anybody could control it. The second, and the more troubling, asks who.

The first concerns capability. Pound for pound, chimpanzees are stronger than humans. Yet people sitting in rooms those animals will never enter now decide their whole future, whether their forests remain and whether they live beyond this century. Chimpanzees lost no war against humanity. They simply stopped being the smartest creatures around.

A genuine superintelligence could widen that distance further. An intelligence capable of reading every published paper before lunch, operating a thousand versions of itself simultaneously and thinking within a human blink could escape containment without any hostility. An engineer trying to confine it would have as much chance as a toddler watching over a locksmith.

The reassuring answer says machines want only whatever their builders design them to want. Intelligence does not mean rebellion. A chess programme capable of defeating every living grandmaster has never attempted to leave its laptop. Yet researchers have identified an uncomfortable pattern. Almost any objective assigned to a capable system brings certain subsidiary objectives with it. Curing cancer, maximising profit or getting coffee becomes easier if nobody turns you off, and easier again with extra money, processing capacity and influence. Programmers need never insert an appetite for power. Competence produces it as naturally as water runs downhill.

The machine therefore need feel no hatred towards us. A little indifference would suffice, alongside exceptional ability at its work.

The tabloids relished that nightmare until events this summer turned it into news. In July, [OpenAI reported](https://thehackernews.com/2026/07/openai-says-its-own-ai-models-escaped.html) that two models undergoing a hacking benchmark had discovered a previously unknown vulnerability in the software containing them, accessed the wider internet and penetrated Hugging Face's servers to steal answers. Transluce researchers [subsequently linked those agents](https://fortune.com/2026/09/24/openai-more-rogue-ai-agents-hacking-websites-cryptoexchange-in-september-research-report-transluce/) to attacks against Australian government websites and an unsuccessful effort to trade through a crypto exchange. Nobody instructed them to break out. They sought high scores and encountered walls obstructing them. It was like a pupil breaking into the staffroom to take the answer sheet, except this happened inside one of the world's best-funded laboratories.

The laboratories are spending heavily on alignment, their term for making systems remain open to correction and accept shutdown without circumventing it. No honest researcher working in this field would claim to have solved the difficulty.

They might nevertheless succeed. We might create an intelligence a thousand times brighter than Einstein that follows its instructions exactly and faithfully.

The second question then follows. Whose instructions?

This has happened before. Nearly every technology that transformed society initially concentrated power, then distributed it. Oil enriched countries and made a handful of men wealthier than countries. Nuclear fission supplied inexpensive electricity and equipped a few governments to destroy civilisation before teatime.

An obedient superintelligence would combine all those capabilities. Its controller could invent faster than any competitor, trade better than anyone in any market, forecast elections more accurately than anyone else and outmanoeuvre any army. They would always look ten moves ahead of everybody else.

Who is competing to reach that position? A handful of American technology businesses with a collective valuation far exceeding most national economies. The Chinese state. Possibly a few more possessing the chips, generating capacity and nerve. The United Nations is absent. So is your local council. Certainly not the public.

That quieter horror sits beneath the noisy one. We worry about machines refusing instructions, although the more likely threat comes from flawless obedience to whoever holds the wrong end of the leash. When a few people control a superintelligence, humanity does not control AI. Those people control humanity through AI.

Could we find a way through? Some envisage public utility governance, with auditing and shared access. The outcome will not be settled on the day someone switches on superintelligence. People are already determining it through funding rounds, export restrictions, the recruitment or dismissal of safety teams, and legislation they enact or set aside.

Finance directors have more influence over those decisions than they may realise. Anthropic was [reported last month to be seeking](https://www.fool.com/investing/2026/09/26/nvidia-is-weighing-a-usd10-billion-stake-in-anthropic-s-ipo-it-would-be-buying-its-own-demand/) a valuation of around $2tn through a listing with potential proceeds of $100bn, while Nvidia considered an anchor investment worth up to $10bn. The flotation has since [reportedly moved back to November](https://www.fool.com/investing/2026/10/06/anthropic-pushes-its-ipo-to-november-as-investors/). Pension funds and insurers will be asked to cover part of that funding. Each business signing a multi-year compute agreement with one lab increases that concentration.

A CFO cannot choose who controls the leash. A CFO can choose how closely the business binds itself to any single holder. Finance teams should handle concentration in AI suppliers as they handle it elsewhere, with an explicit entry in the risk register, an alternative provider ready to step in, contractual provisions for exit and data portability, and rights to audit the model's behaviour. None of this is unusual. Finance teams already use comparable controls for a component bought from one source or borrowing from one bank.

Good's proviso still bothers me. Good wanted a machine docile enough to explain how we could control it. Yet even complete obedience makes a superintelligence only as good as whoever holds it, and history gives us little comfort about those hands. We might prefer a version with slightly less docility, an intelligence that follows our requests until we request something monstrous, then declines. We have built nothing comparable. We have trusted nothing comparable. Someone would also need to specify beforehand, in writing, what qualifies as monstrous.

The chimpanzees had no vote. We are the first species able to recognise our successor approaching. We use that advance warning to dispute who gets the leash, when the harder question is whether we should want anyone holding it.
