## The slogan leaders quote but rarely design for

"Culture eats strategy for breakfast" gets pinned on Peter Drucker, whether he said it or not. It survives because people recognise themselves in it. Neat slideware on one side, messy reality on the other.

Anyone who has sat through a strategy away-day and then watched the next quarter unfold knows the feeling. The words are right. The logic stacks. And back inside the business, people quietly carry on doing whatever has always felt safest.

## Culture is the container, not the enemy

The usual framing treats culture as something that occasionally gets in the way of an otherwise solid plan. Wrong way round. Culture is the container the plan has to live in. If the plan doesn't fit, the container wins. Every time.

Watch what happens when a change hinges on someone making a call — and the call never quite comes. The analysis is done. The trade-offs have been argued to death. Then the decision gets parked for "one more discussion". People stop waiting. They improvise around the uncertainty, and those improvisations harden into how things really get done round here.

By then culture isn't blocking the strategy. It has replaced it.

## The real constraint: what leadership will live with

Every organisation has values on the wall. What actually shapes culture is what leadership is prepared to tolerate when intervening would be awkward.

It shows up in the pattern of exceptions. The role that's "almost right" but never strengthened. The repeated miss that keeps getting explained away. The process everyone agrees is broken and nobody owns. None of these justifies a board-level row on its own — which is exactly why they're dangerous. Together they reset what "acceptable" means.

People aren't stupid. They watch the exceptions, not the values poster, and they draw the obvious conclusion: the stated strategy matters, but not as much as keeping things comfortable.

## Decision-avoidance is a cultural force

Organisations spend enormous energy worrying about bad decisions. The quieter risk is the decisions that never get made at all.

The rhythm is familiar. A thorny issue lands on the agenda, generates good debate, then slides into another working group, another paper, another cycle. Nobody does anything obviously wrong. But the organisation learns something precise: wait long enough and most hard calls dissolve into "let's see how it plays out".

Once that lesson lands, the rational move is to manage sideways. Effort flows towards the informal power map — because that's where things actually move — and away from the declared priorities.

## You can't make people do it. You can make it make sense.

Most strategy writing carries a hidden assumption: explain the plan clearly enough and execution follows. It doesn't, and everyone who has watched a plan die in the operating rhythm knows it.

On the ground, people are running simpler calculations. Will this help me do my job? Will anyone notice if I do the harder thing? Am I exposed if it goes wrong? If following the strategy means more friction and more risk for less recognition, no active resistance is needed. Effort just drains towards the path of least pain.

The reliable route is duller and harder: design things so that behaving in line with the strategy is easier — and better rewarded — than ignoring it. When the right behaviour is also the practical, socially safe one, compliance stops being something you chase. It turns up on its own.

## Execution asset or execution drag

Culture gets called "soft". The effects are anything but, and you can see them in the numbers without ever putting the word on a line in the P&L.

When culture helps execution: decisions move, escalations are clean, projects actually close, forecast variance narrows for reasons that have nothing to do with clever modelling. When it drags: duplication, slow resolution, half-finished initiatives, and the same handful of reliable people asked to "just get it over the line" one more time.

None of it arrives as a single shock. It compounds — tolerances and delays stacking quietly over months, then years.

## Design strategies your culture can digest

The practical move is to stop treating culture as a side issue and accept it as the operating environment the strategy has to survive in.

Concretely: be honest about which behaviours are genuinely non-negotiable, and back that honesty when it's inconvenient. Make the calls that shape how work really gets done — staffing, standards, ownership — instead of leaving them in the long grass. Align recognition and progression with the standards the strategy actually depends on, not just the outputs that happen to land.

And treat inaction as a choice, because it is one. From a finance perspective the cost of a delayed decision is every bit as real as the cost of a bad one. It's just harder to attribute. The organisation learns from it either way.

## The quiet job description

On paper, leadership sets direction. In practice, it's equally about deciding what gets reinforced, what gets challenged, and what quietly slides.

Nobody ever made people live a strategy by saying it more loudly. What can be built is an environment where following it feels coherent, fair and worth the effort — the point at which culture and strategy stop fighting and start doing the same job.

Then culture isn't waiting to eat the strategy for breakfast. It's doing something far more useful: carrying it into the dozens of unglamorous decisions where, viewed from the CFO's desk, execution actually happens.
